It is dawn on the Pacific coast and you are standing ankle deep in the surf. The water is cold. The sound is the one you have heard all your life, the long hiss of a wave pulling back over sand. Then the water keeps pulling back. It does not return. It runs out past your feet, past the rocks, past the end of the pier, and it does not stop at the horizon. Within seconds the surf falls silent. In front of you lies wet sand, then bare rock, then a slope that tips down and away into a darkness the sun has never reached. Behind you, the Pacific Ocean is gone.

Before anything else, let us be plain. This cannot happen. No known process in physics removes half of the world's ocean in an instant, and there is nowhere for that much water to go. So for this video the water simply vanishes, and then every law of physics stays switched on. We will follow what happens in seconds, in minutes, in hours, in days, and in years. Near the end there is a turn that almost nobody sees coming, because the real story of the Pacific is stranger than the scenario.

First, how big is the hole you are looking into? The Pacific covers about 165 million square kilometres. That is almost a third of the whole surface of the planet. It holds roughly 670 million cubic kilometres of water, which is about half of all the ocean water on Earth. Its average depth is around four kilometres. At its deepest point, the Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench, the floor lies nearly eleven kilometres down. Mount Everest is about 8.8 kilometres tall. You could drop it into that trench and its summit would still sit more than two kilometres below the old surface.

Hold on to that number, the one about half of all the ocean. It matters more than the size of the hole. In a few minutes you will see why the rest of the world is about to be pulled into this story whether it likes it or not.

Start with the first ten seconds. Along most coasts the sea floor does not drop away at once. It slopes gently outward across the continental shelf, which typically ends at a depth of a little over a hundred metres. From where you stand, that shelf is now a wide, flat plain of mud, shell and gravel. It is littered with fish, crabs and kelp, all of it stranded, all of it still wet. The smell hits you first. It is the smell of a low tide, but stretched across an entire ocean.

Then you reach the edge. Past the shelf comes the continental slope, and there the world falls away. Depending on the coast, the ground drops thousands of metres over a few dozen kilometres. It is not a sheer cliff everywhere, but in places it is steep enough that you would not want to stand near it. Canyons cut into it, some as large as the Grand Canyon, and until a moment ago they were full of water. You are standing at the rim of the largest depression on Earth, and you can see only the first few kilometres of it.

Now something happens that you cannot see. Air is falling into the hole.

Think about what the atmosphere is. It is a layer of gas held down by gravity, thickest at the bottom. At the old sea level, the air presses on you with a force we call one atmosphere. If the ground drops away, the air follows it downward and gets denser as it goes. A simple model of the atmosphere, the one pilots and engineers use, says that four kilometres below the old sea level the pressure would be about one and a half times what you feel on the beach. At the bottom of the Challenger Deep it would be a little over three times. You would not need a diving suit to survive that pressure, though you would feel it in your ears on the way down.

That is the first surprise of the scenario, and it comes with a cost. Air is not free. To fill a volume of 670 million cubic kilometres, the atmosphere has to give up mass. A rough calculation, using that standard atmosphere and the volume of the lost water, suggests the new hole would swallow something like a fifth of the entire atmosphere. This is a back of the envelope estimate, not a simulation. But it points to something odd. Once the air settles, the pressure at the old sea level would be perhaps three quarters of what it was. That is like standing at an altitude of around two kilometres. The beach where you started would feel like a mountain town.

Stay with me, because the air is not the biggest problem. In about a minute, the biggest problem arrives from the other side of the planet.

Water responds to a missing neighbour the way a crowd responds to an open door. The Atlantic, the Indian Ocean and the Southern Ocean are all connected to the Pacific, and every one of them is suddenly sitting higher than the empty basin next to it. Water starts to move. The speed of a disturbance in deep water is set by depth. For a long wave, it equals the square root of gravity times depth. At four kilometres, that comes to roughly 700 kilometres an hour. That is the speed at which a tsunami crosses an open ocean. In 1960, the great Chile earthquake sent a tsunami across the Pacific, and it reached Japan after about a day.

But what is coming now is not a tsunami. A tsunami is a ripple on a full ocean. This is an ocean falling into an empty basin, a dam break so large that no engineer has a name for it. The idealised theory of a dam break says the leading edge of the flow races ahead at some hundreds of metres per second. Real water is slower, because it has to squeeze through the gaps between continents. And those gaps are what decide the next few years.

Which brings us to the first thing to watch. Where does the water actually get in? The Bering Strait is far too shallow and narrow to matter. The seas of Indonesia are crowded with islands and shallow shelves. The real gateways are the wide, deep passages around the southern tip of South America and south of Australia, where the Southern Ocean touches the Pacific. The strongest current on Earth, the Antarctic Circumpolar Current, already flows through that region, and it carries more than 100 million cubic metres of water every second. In our scenario, that is a gentle stream. Hold that thought, because the size of the flood is about to be put in context.

Move forward to the first hours. You have left the beach and climbed a hill, and from there you can see what the coast has become. The water is not simply sitting still. Every sea connected to the Pacific is draining into it. The Sea of Japan, the Gulf of California and the East China Sea are all emptying. The pier near you is now a bridge across dry sand, with its legs standing a hundred metres above the ground.

Here, the arithmetic becomes impossible to avoid. Half of the ocean is gone. The other half, about 670 million cubic kilometres, will not stay in the Atlantic and the Indian Ocean. It will spread out over the whole of the ocean basin, including the Pacific. If you spread that remaining water evenly across the area of the old ocean, you get a depth of roughly half of today's average, which is about 3.7 kilometres. So the sea would end up around 1.8 kilometres deep on average. That means sea level, everywhere on Earth, falls by something like one and a half to two kilometres. The number is rough, because the sea floor is not a neat bowl, but the scale is not in doubt.

Think about what that does to the world's coastlines. Every harbour on Earth, from Rotterdam to Singapore to Los Angeles, ends up stranded far above the new waterline. About 80 percent of world trade by volume travels by sea. Every one of those routes is cut, not by a blockade but by geography. The ships that were at sea when the Pacific vanished are now sitting on the sea floor, some of them on slopes, tipped over. The ships in Atlantic ports will be left on dry land within hours as the water drains away from them.

And the shorelines move dramatically. Remember the last ice age, when so much water was locked up in ice sheets that sea level stood around 120 metres lower than today. A walkable land bridge joined Siberia to Alaska. Great plains of exposed shelf joined islands in Southeast Asia into a single landmass. Our scenario is more than ten times larger. The land that appears is not just shelf. It is the sloping flanks of continents, and the tops of mountain ranges that have always been underwater.

Here is a reason to keep watching. The most dangerous thing in this story is not the water. It is what the missing water does to the weather, and for that we need to go from hours to days.

In the first days, the air above the basin starts to heat. When air sinks, it is compressed, and compressed air warms. In the standard atmosphere, temperature rises about 6.5 degrees Celsius for every kilometre you descend. If you extend that model down, you get a temperature around 40 degrees Celsius at four kilometres below the old sea level, and above 80 degrees at the bottom of the trenches. The real air would not follow that simple rule. Winds would mix it, and the cold bottom water that was once down there is gone. But the basic message stands. The deepest parts of the Pacific floor would be among the hottest places on land.

Now think about what the ocean was doing for the climate, because it was doing a lot. Water absorbs heat extremely well. The top few metres of the ocean hold as much heat as the entire atmosphere above it. The oceans have also taken up about 90 percent of the extra heat trapped by greenhouse gases in recent decades. They store it, move it, and release it slowly. Take away half of that, and one of the planet's largest thermal buffers disappears. Winters get colder in the interior of continents, and summers get hotter. The weather gets less gentle.

Then there is rain. The oceans supply about 90 percent of the water that evaporates into the atmosphere. With half the ocean gone and the remaining surface shrunk as the shorelines retreat, there is much less evaporation. Rain depends on it. We have never run a climate model on a world like this, so what I am giving you is reasoning, not prediction. But the reasoning points one way. Most of the land gets drier, and the monsoons, which depend on warm ocean water feeding moist air onto the continents, are in trouble. Billions of people depend on those rains.

Now a claim you have probably heard. You may have been told that the ocean makes half of our oxygen. NOAA says that at least half of the oxygen produced each year comes from the ocean, mostly from tiny photosynthesising plankton. That is true as far as it goes. But whether it matters for the oxygen you breathe is contested, because most of that oxygen is used up again in the ocean by the creatures that eat the plankton. The net contribution is much smaller. And the atmosphere holds an enormous stock of oxygen already. So you will not run out of air. What you lose is food. The plankton and the fish that depend on them are a large part of the food chain, and the shallow waters where most marine life lives are now dry land.

The ocean also takes up about a quarter of the carbon dioxide we emit each year. That sink would shrink, and the carbon dioxide would stay in the air. The effect would not be immediate, but it points in the direction of a warmer world, at the very moment the lost water makes the climate harder to predict.

There is one more reason to stay with this, and it concerns the ground itself. Because the next part of the story takes years, and it begins with the Earth's crust rising up.

The weight of four kilometres of water pushes down on the sea floor. Water is not heavy compared to rock, but four kilometres of it is a great deal of load. When you remove that load, the crust floats upward, slowly, on the hot, soft rock beneath it. Geologists call this isostatic rebound. You can see it today in places where the great ice sheets melted. Around Hudson Bay in Canada, the land is still rising by roughly a centimetre a year, more than ten thousand years after the ice went away.

Now compare. Water is about a third as dense as the rock of the mantle. So removing four kilometres of water should eventually lift the sea floor by something on the order of a kilometre. That is a rough estimate, and it would take many thousands of years to finish. But it would begin at once. The Pacific basin would slowly become shallower, not because it fills with sediment, but because the planet pushes back.

Here is something that should make you uneasy. The Pacific is ringed by the Ring of Fire, a belt of subduction zones that hosts about 75 percent of the world's active volcanoes and about 90 percent of its earthquakes. Those faults are held in balance by the pressure of rock and water. Change the load, and you change the stress. We know from the end of the last ice age that rapid changes in surface load can alter how often volcanoes erupt. Some studies find that volcanic activity increased after the ice melted. The link is debated, and no one can say how a change this sudden would play out on the Ring of Fire. But it is the kind of question that geologists do not like to leave open.

Then the tides. The Moon is moving away from Earth at about 3.8 centimetres a year, a figure measured by bouncing lasers off mirrors left on the lunar surface. That drift is driven largely by tides, and tides are driven by the shape and size of the oceans. Change the oceans this drastically and you change the tides. How they would change, nobody can say with confidence. The oceans would be smaller, shallower in places, and broken up by new land. It is likely that the rhythm of the sea, which every coastal creature relies on, would be rewritten.

So how long does the sea take to settle? Let us do some honest arithmetic. To reach the same level everywhere, roughly 300 million cubic kilometres of water has to move into the Pacific basin from elsewhere. That is about 3 times ten to the seventeenth cubic metres. Suppose the flow through the gateways could reach ten times the greatest flood we have evidence for in Earth's history, which I will explain in a moment. That is a billion cubic metres a second. At that rate, the refilling takes about ten years. At a tenth of that rate, it takes a century. So the answer is somewhere between a decade and a century, set entirely by how fast the narrow gates between the oceans can let water through.

Now, that flood I mentioned. It is not a thought experiment. It happened.

About six million years ago, the Mediterranean Sea was cut off from the Atlantic, probably by tectonic movement closing the gap at Gibraltar. Geologists call the episode the Messinian Salinity Crisis. It lasted from about 5.96 to 5.33 million years ago. With no new water coming in, the Mediterranean evaporated faster than rivers could refill it. It left behind a layer of salt that in places is up to two kilometres thick, buried under the modern sea floor. How much of the sea actually dried out is contested. Some researchers think the basin was almost completely empty, with the sea level dropping by a kilometre and a half or more. Others think it was only partly drained, with deep brine pools left behind. The debate has gone on for decades.

What is better understood is how it ended. About 5.33 million years ago, the barrier at Gibraltar broke. Water from the Atlantic poured into the empty basin. Modelling by Daniel Garcia-Castellanos and colleagues, published in Nature in 2009, suggests the flood peaked at around 100 million cubic metres per second. The Amazon, the largest river on Earth, carries about 200 thousand cubic metres per second. So this was hundreds of times larger. And the same work suggests the whole basin refilled in somewhere between a few months and two years. That is the closest thing our planet has to a rehearsal for what you are watching. The Mediterranean is a tiny puddle compared with the Pacific, and still it produced the biggest flood we know of.

Now let us step back, because we have been treating the lost Pacific as a disaster, and we have not yet asked the question that makes this scenario more than a thought experiment. Where does the Pacific actually go?

Here is the turn. The Pacific is already draining. You just cannot see it, because it is happening in slow motion and out of sight.

The ocean floor is not permanent. It is created at mid-ocean ridges, where new crust rises and spreads. And it is destroyed at subduction zones, where one plate dives beneath another and sinks into the mantle. The Pacific is ringed by those zones. They are why the Ring of Fire exists. The floor of the Pacific is being consumed around its edges faster than the East Pacific Rise can make new floor. As a result, the Pacific is shrinking while the Atlantic, which has no such ring, is widening at roughly two and a half centimetres a year. The oldest ocean floor on Earth is about 180 million years old, and much of it is in the western Pacific. Older floor than that has already been swallowed. In the geological sense, the ocean is eating itself.

Where will it end? There is a real scientific disagreement here, and I want to be honest about it. One idea, proposed by a team at Yale in 2012, is that the Pacific will keep shrinking until it closes entirely. The Americas would collide with Asia, forming a new supercontinent called Amasia, somewhere between 50 and 200 million years from now. Another idea says the Atlantic will close first, bringing the continents together into a supercontinent sometimes called Pangaea Ultima. A third says the Pacific will outlast them all. No one knows. Plate tectonics is hard to run forward in time. But in all of these, the Pacific as we know it does not last. One day, the Pacific Ocean really will be drained. Only it will take more than fifty million years, and no one will be on the beach to see it.

And there is a second drain, deeper still. Each subducting plate carries water with it, locked in the minerals of the old sea floor. Some of that water comes back up through volcanoes. But some of it goes far down, into a layer of the mantle between about 410 and 660 kilometres below the surface, called the transition zone. In 2014, researchers reported a tiny diamond from Brazil that contained a speck of a mineral called ringwoodite, which is normally found only at those depths. The mineral held about one and a half percent water by weight. That is a single sample, and whether it is typical is debated. But if it is, then the deep Earth could hold several times as much water as all of the oceans combined. Some scientists suspect it does. Others suspect it holds far less. The question is open, and it is one of the most interesting open questions in Earth science.

So think again about the scenario we started with. We asked what happens if the Pacific vanishes in an instant. The answer is a catastrophe on a scale no species has faced, with thin air, searing heat in the basin, stranded ports, failing monsoons, and a flood that takes years or decades to finish. But the deeper answer is that oceans do vanish, and they have before. The Mediterranean did, at least in part. The floor of the Pacific has been vanishing for 180 million years, a little at a time, into the Earth. The water you were standing in that morning has been through this before, and it will go through it again. The only thing that was impossible was the speed.

You are still standing on that hill. The sun is higher now, and the plain below you is dry. The air feels thin and a little too warm, and somewhere far off, the first edge of a very large flood is on its way. If this had been real, you would have had a few hours to decide where to go.

So here is my question for you. If you had those few hours, and you knew everything you now know about the air, the heat, the dry ports and the flood on its way, where would you go, and what is the first thing you would take with you? Tell us in the comments, and tell us what you think we got wrong.
