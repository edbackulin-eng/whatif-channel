You are standing on a bare hillside, and the sky is clear. A minute ago nothing was wrong. Now a point of light has appeared above the horizon, and it is brightening faster than anything in the sky should. It is already brighter than the sun. It makes no sound at all. You have about eight seconds. Count them with me. One. Two. Three.

Hold that count, because we will come back to it. First, a plain statement. This cannot happen. The rock in this story is real. Its name is Apophis, and it really will pass close to Earth. But it will not hit us in twenty twenty-nine, or in twenty thirty-six, or in twenty sixty-eight, and NASA's current analysis finds no impact risk from it for at least the next hundred years. So everything that follows is a thought experiment. We take the real asteroid and the real numbers, we nudge it one step sideways onto us, and we leave the physics switched on.

Here is what we are working with. Apophis is a stony asteroid about three hundred and forty metres across, which is roughly as wide as the Eiffel Tower is tall. Radar and light measurements put its mass at around sixty million tonnes. On a collision course it would hit at about twelve kilometres every second, which is more than thirty times the speed of sound. Take half the mass times the speed squared, and the energy comes out near twelve hundred megatons of TNT. The most powerful nuclear weapon ever tested, the Soviet Tsar Bomba in nineteen sixty-one, was about fifty megatons. So this is roughly twenty-four of those, delivered at one spot, in a fraction of a second. Or, if you prefer, something like eighty thousand Hiroshima bombs.

And yet there is a way of looking at that number that makes it seem almost small. I will show you near the end, and it changes what the real danger turns out to be.

To see why anyone worried at all, go back to June of two thousand four. Astronomers at Kitt Peak in Arizona find a faint dot moving against the stars. It is later named Apophis, after the Egyptian serpent of chaos. By December its orbit is still rough, and the early numbers are ugly. For a few days, the odds of a hit on the thirteenth of April, twenty twenty-nine, climb to about one in thirty-seven. That earns a four on the Torino scale, the highest rating any object has ever received, and a four means a close encounter with at least a one percent chance of a collision capable of regional devastation. Then, on the twenty-seventh of December, astronomers find older photographs of the same dot, taken in March of that year. The orbit tightens, and the risk for twenty twenty-nine drops to zero. Keep that date in mind. The thirteenth of April, twenty twenty-nine, is a Friday.

The story was not over. In the real world, Apophis will pass about thirty-two thousand kilometres from the centre of the Earth. That is inside the ring of geostationary satellites, and about one twelfth of the way to the Moon. People across Europe, Africa and western Asia will be able to see it without a telescope, as a moving point of light. Earth's gravity will bend its path, and for years there was a worry about a patch of space called a gravitational keyhole, only a few hundred metres wide. Had the rock threaded it, it would have returned in twenty thirty-six on a collision course. Later observations showed it misses. Radar measurements in March of twenty twenty-one then cleared twenty sixty-eight as well. So in the real world, we are safe. Now let us stop being safe.

Start the clock. Apophis crosses a hundred kilometres of altitude, the line most people call the edge of space. Straight down, it has about eight seconds of atmosphere ahead of it. Here is the first surprise. The atmosphere barely matters. Above every square metre of ground sits about ten tonnes of air. Take a column of air as wide as Apophis, and everything in that column weighs about one sixtieth as much as the rock. The asteroid is sixty times heavier than the air in its way. It cannot be stopped.

That is not true of smaller rocks. In February of twenty thirteen, a stone about twenty metres wide burst high above Chelyabinsk in Russia with the energy of roughly five hundred kilotons, and the blast broke enough windows to injure more than a thousand people. In nineteen oh eight, something perhaps fifty or sixty metres wide flattened about two thousand square kilometres of Siberian forest at Tunguska. Scientists still argue about its energy, with estimates running from about three to thirty megatons. Apophis is far bigger than either. It arrives at the ground with almost all its energy intact.

In the air ahead, the squeezed gas cannot slip aside. It is compressed into a shock front hotter than the surface of the sun, and it blazes. Behind the rock, a wake of glowing air stretches back to the top of the sky. The whole landscape lights up with a harsh, flat, white glare. Four. Five. Six. Seven. The glare is getting hotter on your skin. Eight.

Contact. The front of the rock hits the ground at twelve kilometres a second. The back of it is still more than three hundred metres up in the sky. It takes under a tenth of a second for the whole asteroid to be stopped, and all that motion has to go somewhere. It becomes heat and shock. At the point of contact, the pressure is millions of times that of the atmosphere, and the rock and the ground beneath it do not so much break as melt and vaporise. Apophis is mostly stone with some metal, so there is nothing in it that could survive that.

Over the next few tens of seconds, a bowl opens in the Earth. A tall curtain of molten and shattered rock rises around it. At the most likely impact angle, forty-five degrees, standard impact scaling gives a temporary cavity about four kilometres wide and more than a kilometre deep. As the walls slump inward, the final crater is on the order of four to five kilometres across. For comparison, Meteor Crater in Arizona is about one point two kilometres wide, and it was dug about fifty thousand years ago by an iron meteorite only around fifty metres across. Apophis would dig something several times wider.

But the crater is not what hurts you from a hundred kilometres away. What reaches you is a matter of sequence, and it is not the sequence films show. Stay with me, because the order is the whole point.

Back on the hillside, a hundred kilometres from the impact. First comes the light, which arrives instantly. The heat pulse is brief, but at tens of kilometres it is enough to ignite dry grass and scorch bare skin. Then comes the ground. Shaking travels through rock at several kilometres per second, so within about half a minute the hillside begins to heave, with something like a major earthquake, and seismometers on every continent will record it. Then, and only then, comes the air. A blast wave moves at roughly the speed of sound, about three hundred and forty metres per second. At a hundred kilometres, that means it arrives almost five minutes after the flash. For those five minutes you are standing in a silent, shaking world, looking at a column of dust and fire rising over the horizon, and the blast is still coming. When it hits, it can shatter windows more than a hundred kilometres from the crater, and within a few tens of kilometres of the impact, almost anything people have built is flattened.

Meanwhile, the debris thrown out of the crater comes back down. Closest in, it falls as boulders and hot gravel, and finer material is thrown far higher, where it spreads on the winds. Scientists differ on how far a burning rain of ejecta would reach for an impact of this size, and on how many fires it would start. What they agree on is that the destruction is regional. Hold onto that word, regional, because the next scenario puts it under pressure.

Here is the odd fact about where Apophis would most likely land. About seventy-one percent of Earth's surface is ocean. The average depth is roughly three point seven kilometres. A rock three hundred and forty metres wide does not care. It punches through the water in a fraction of a second, boiling a column of seawater into steam, and cuts a cavity in the sea several kilometres across. The cavity collapses, a plume of water and vapour is thrown high into the sky, and the sea sends out waves.

What those waves do next is genuinely contested. Early work in the year two thousand, by Steven Ward and Erik Asphaug, suggested that asteroid impacts could send dangerous tsunamis across whole ocean basins. Later computer simulations, including work at Los Alamos National Laboratory, found that impact waves are short and steep, and that they lose energy quickly as they spread, unlike the long waves of an earthquake. On that view, the damage is severe near the impact and much weaker far away. Nobody has watched a real one, so nobody can say for certain which camp is right.

Now speed up. In the first hours, the sky downwind of the impact darkens with fine dust and smoke. Rock vapour from the fireball cools and condenses into tiny droplets, and some of it is thrown above most of the weather. The fires set by the heat pulse and the falling debris merge into larger ones. Hospitals within hundreds of kilometres are overwhelmed, and roads are blocked by wreckage. And this is where the physics hands the story over to something else, which is warning. I will come back to it, because it is the part that surprised me most.

In the days after, the picture is local, and it is bleak. The flattened zone is many times larger than the two thousand square kilometres of Tunguska, though how many times depends on which Tunguska energy you trust and whether the impact is on land or at sea. Burning forests send smoke across the region. Satellite images show a fresh scar, the crater, ringed by a blanket of ejecta. But the planet itself carries on. Earth's weather, its oceans and its crust barely register the event. Why not? Because of scale, and we will look at that scale properly in a moment.

Years. The key question is climate. Scientists often use an asteroid about one kilometre wide as the rough threshold for global damage, the point at which enough dust and soot reach the upper atmosphere to dim the sun across the whole planet. Apophis is about a third of that width. Because mass grows with the cube of the width, it carries about twenty-five times less mass. Some models of impacts this size predict a brief and modest cooling. Others predict almost nothing you could measure. For comparison, the eruption of Mount Pinatubo in nineteen ninety-one cooled the planet by about half a degree Celsius for a year or two, and the eruption of Tambora in eighteen fifteen was followed by the year without a summer. No impact of Apophis's size has ever been observed, so there is no direct data. The honest answer is that the global climate effect is probably small, and that it is unsettled.

To place Apophis on the ladder, think of the asteroid that ended the age of the dinosaurs about sixty-six million years ago. It was around ten kilometres wide, and its energy was tens of thousands of times greater than Apophis's. That is a different kind of event, one that changes the whole planet. Apophis is a catastrophe with a boundary.

Over decades, the crater fills with water and sediment, and a ring of rubble marks the spot. In the geological record it would be a minor event. Objects this size strike the Earth only about once every tens of thousands of years, according to NASA's estimates, which means nothing like it has happened in all of recorded human history. And that brings us to the turn I promised.

First, the number I teased. The Sun delivers about one hundred and seventy quadrillion watts to the Earth. Divide the energy of Apophis, close to five billion billion joules, by that, and you get about twenty-eight seconds. The entire destructive energy of this asteroid is about half a minute of ordinary sunshine falling on the whole planet. It is only terrifying because it all arrives in a few seconds, on a patch of ground a few kilometres wide. The danger of an asteroid is not its size. It is its concentration.

Second, the part that surprised me. Everything I have described, the crater, the blast, the fire, is easy to calculate and impossible to stop once the rock is in the air. But almost none of it has to touch a person. An asteroid is the only natural disaster we can predict years ahead, to within a few kilometres, and the arithmetic of avoiding it is almost absurdly gentle. Earth moves around the Sun at nearly thirty kilometres every second, which is about eighteen hundred kilometres each minute. Once you account for Earth's gravity pulling the rock in, our effective target is roughly twenty-seven thousand kilometres wide. So if Apophis arrives somewhere between a few minutes and a quarter of an hour early or late, depending on how squarely it was aimed, the Earth has moved out of the way, and nothing happens at all.

How much of a push is that? Done a decade early, a change in the asteroid's speed of about a centimetre or two per second would do it, and the rock is already moving around the Sun at tens of kilometres per second. That is less than one part in a million.

We have already done a small version of this. In September of twenty twenty-two, NASA's DART spacecraft, about five hundred and seventy kilograms, flew into Dimorphos, a moonlet roughly one hundred and sixty metres wide, at about six kilometres per second. The moonlet's orbit around its partner Didymos shrank from eleven hours and fifty-five minutes to about eleven hours and twenty-three minutes, a change of thirty-two minutes. The speed change was only a few millimetres per second, and the debris thrown off gave an extra shove, roughly three to four times larger than the spacecraft alone would have delivered. Apophis is about fourteen times more massive than Dimorphos, so by a back-of-the-envelope estimate, it would take on the order of a hundred such impacts, or a better tool, or, and this is the key, a decade or more of lead time. Time is the cheap ingredient. Warning is the weapon.

Which brings us back to the hillside, and to the count. In this thought experiment, you would almost certainly not be standing there. A rock this size would be found long before it came, its path would be known to within a few kilometres, and everyone in the blast zone would be moved out well in advance. The physics of an Apophis strike is brutal. The politics, in the end, might be harder. Who decides which country gets the crater? Where do millions of people go? Nobody has a plan, because nobody has ever needed one.

There is one last twist, and it comes from the real timeline. Before the flyby, Apophis circles the Sun in a little under a year. After it, Earth's gravity will have stretched its orbit to something longer than a year. The tug may also change how it spins. Apophis tumbles, taking about thirty and a half hours to complete one cycle, and some researchers expect our gravity to alter that tumble and even shake loose rock from its surface in landslides. Others expect little change. It is a live debate. NASA's OSIRIS-APEX spacecraft is due to arrive soon after the flyby to find out. The rock that comes out of that encounter will not be quite the rock that went in.

So here is my question. Suppose we knew, a decade ahead, exactly where a three hundred and forty metre rock was going to land, and we had two options that cost about the same. We could spend the money on a spacecraft to push it away, with some chance of failure. Or we could spend it moving everyone out of the blast zone, which would work, but would cost people their homes. Which would you choose? And what would you do if the hillside you live on was in the middle of the circle? Tell me in the comments.
