You are falling feet first, and nothing hurts. Above you, the sky has shrunk into a bright, crowded circle of stars. Below you is a patch of black so complete that it looks less like an object and more like a hole cut out of the universe. You are moving faster than anything human has ever moved. You have no engine, no rope, and no way back. In a short while you will cross a line that nothing returns from. And here is the strange part. When you cross it, you will not feel a thing. The line is not where the danger lives. The danger is somewhere else, and near the end I will show you why even that comforting story might be wrong.

Let us be plain about the premise first. You cannot do this. The nearest black hole we have confirmed is a system called Gaia BH1, about one thousand five hundred and sixty light years from Earth. The fastest spacecraft ever built is the Parker Solar Probe, which at its closest pass to the Sun reached about one hundred and ninety-two kilometres per second. At that speed, the trip to Gaia BH1 would take roughly two and a half million years. Nobody can survive that, and nothing we have could carry them. So this is a thought experiment. We leave the physics switched on, we deliver you by magic, and we watch what the universe does.

I am going to drop you into three different black holes, because the size of the hole decides how you die, and when. The small one is like Cygnus X-1, with about twenty-one times the mass of the Sun. The medium one is Sagittarius A star, the black hole at the centre of our own galaxy, with about four million times the mass of the Sun. The giant is M87 star, the first black hole ever photographed, by the Event Horizon Telescope in 2019, with about six and a half billion times the mass of the Sun. The small one is the cruel one, and we start there. But first, you need to know what you are falling toward.

A black hole is a region where gravity is so strong that the speed you would need to escape is faster than light. The boundary of that region is called the event horizon. It is not a surface. There is no wall, no skin, nothing to touch. It is a one-way boundary in space and time. For a black hole that does not spin, the radius of the horizon grows in direct proportion to its mass. Squeeze the Sun into a black hole and the horizon would have a radius of about three kilometres. Squeeze the Earth, and it would be about nine millimetres. Size is the whole secret here, and in a few minutes it will decide whether you live for a third of a millisecond or for a full day.

Here are the sizes of our three holes. Cygnus X-1 has a horizon with a radius of about sixty kilometres, small enough to sit inside a large city. Sagittarius A star has a horizon of about twelve million kilometres. That would fit comfortably inside the orbit of Mercury, which has an average radius of about fifty-eight million kilometres. And M87 star has a horizon of about nineteen billion kilometres, roughly four times the distance from the Sun to Neptune. Same laws, same kind of object, and wildly different experiences for the person falling in. Keep those three numbers in mind, because there is a twist in how they treat your body that almost nobody expects.

Now the fall begins, and the first thing you notice is not darkness. It is light behaving badly. At one and a half times the horizon radius, there is a ring where light itself can be bent into a circle around the hole. Light that leaves your shoulder sideways can curve all the way around and come back to meet you. Closer in, at three times the horizon radius for a hole that does not spin, there is a boundary called the innermost stable orbit. Inside it, nothing can circle steadily. Gas and dust spiral inward and cannot stop. Remember that boundary, because it is the inner edge of something that could kill you before the horizon ever matters.

That something is the accretion disc. In a system like Cygnus X-1, the black hole is stripping gas from a companion star, and that gas piles into a flat, spinning disc. Friction heats it to millions of degrees, and it shines brightly in X-rays. If you fell through that disc, you would be cooked long before anything else happened. So we make a rule. You fall in from above the disc, along the spin axis, where the sky is clear. Sagittarius A star is easier, because its gas is thin and faint, and the disc there barely glows. Still, even on a clean path, the small black hole has a trick waiting for you, and it begins with something your own body has felt all your life.

Gravity gets weaker with distance. Right now, your feet are slightly closer to the Earth than your head, so they are pulled very slightly harder. You never notice, because the difference is tiny. Near a black hole, that difference is called a tidal force, and it can become monstrous. At the horizon of Cygnus X-1, the pull on your feet would exceed the pull on your head by about five million times the gravity you feel standing on Earth. That is a calculation from general relativity, for a body two metres tall. And you do not even get that far. You are stretched to destruction thousands of kilometres from the horizon, long before you reach it.

The physicists who describe this call it spaghettification, and the name is accurate. Your body is pulled along the line toward the hole and squeezed in from the sides, because gravity at the sides points inward toward the centre. You stretch, then you fail, then you become a thin stream of atoms. Your last conscious moments are measured in fractions of a second. The cruelest detail is that the horizon is not even involved. Your remains cross it later, as a ribbon of matter, taking about a third of a millisecond to fall from the horizon to the centre. So with a small black hole, the answer to what you feel at the horizon is that you are already gone. But a bigger hole changes everything, and that is where this gets interesting.

Let us rewind and pick Sagittarius A star instead. It sits about twenty-seven thousand light years from us, in the direction of the constellation Sagittarius. In 2022 the Event Horizon Telescope released its first image of it, a glowing ring around a dark centre, and the measured mass was about four million Suns. Tidal force falls off with the square of the mass, so a heavier hole means a much gentler edge. At the horizon of Sagittarius A star, that stretching across your two metres is about a ten-thousandth of the gravity you feel on Earth. You would not notice it. This is the moment where the story turns, so keep listening.

You reach the horizon, and nothing happens. Einstein's equivalence principle says that if you are in free fall, you feel weightless, and the laws of physics inside your small region look just like they do in empty space. There is no bump, no flash, no wall. If you had your eyes closed, you could not say when you crossed. You would have no instrument in your pocket that could tell you. And yet, from that instant, every possible future for you ends at the centre. You are inside the horizon, and the rest of your life has a length. For Sagittarius A star, the longest it can possibly be is about a minute.

Let us look around, because you have a minute and the view is extraordinary. In computer simulations of this fall, such as those made by the astrophysicist Andrew Hamilton at the University of Colorado Boulder, the picture is not a black sky. Looking up, the entire outside universe is squeezed into a bright patch that shrinks as you fall, its light bent and its colours shifted. Looking down, the dark spot grows. You cannot see the centre ahead of you, because no light from it can reach you in advance. It is not a place you see coming. It is a moment you reach. And what your friends outside see of you is far stranger.

Imagine a friend watching from a safe distance, far from the hole. They see you approach the horizon, but they never see you cross it. Time near a black hole runs slower than time far away, and at the horizon that slowing becomes extreme. Your image appears to freeze and to crawl toward the edge. Meanwhile the light coming from you is stretched to longer and longer wavelengths, so your colour reddens and your glow fades, and within moments you are too dim to see. To your friend, you hang at the edge, fading, forever. To you, you crossed in an instant and felt nothing. Both accounts are standard general relativity, and they are not allowed to contradict each other. Hold that thought, because it comes back at the end.

Now the giant. Swap places and fall into M87 star, fifty-five million light years away in the galaxy Messier 87. The Event Horizon Telescope imaged it in 2019 and measured a mass of about six and a half billion Suns. Its horizon is so large, and gravity so smooth across it, that the tidal force at the edge is utterly negligible. You cross with no hint of stress. And then you have time, more time than you might think. The longest you can possibly last between the horizon and the centre is about a day. A full day of ordinary-feeling weightlessness inside a black hole. What would you do with it? Because the real question is whether you could change your fate.

You cannot. This is the part of black hole physics that is hardest to hold in your head. Inside the horizon, space and time swap their roles. Outside, you can move in any spatial direction you like, and you are forced to move forward in time. Inside, the direction toward the centre becomes the one you are forced along, like time. The centre is not a place ahead of you. It is a time ahead of you. Trying to avoid it is like trying to avoid next Tuesday. No engine, however powerful, can turn you around, because every path that points into the future points toward the centre.

And here is something that surprises almost everyone. You might think the best thing to do is fight. Fire your rockets, push away from the centre, delay the end. It does the opposite. Inside a non-spinning black hole, the longest possible time you can experience is the one you get by doing nothing. Free fall maximises your remaining life. Any thrust, in any direction, shortens it. So the heroic struggle costs you seconds, and the calm acceptance buys you the most. For Sagittarius A star, that means about a minute is the ceiling, and fighting trims it. Nature rewards the passenger and punishes the pilot.

So what is at the end? According to general relativity, the centre is a singularity, a place where the curvature of space and time grows without limit. In 1965, the physicist Roger Penrose proved that once a horizon forms, a singularity is unavoidable in Einstein's theory, and in 2020 that work earned him a share of the Nobel Prize in Physics. But very few physicists think an actual infinity exists there. Most take it as a sign that general relativity breaks down, and that a theory of quantum gravity, which we do not yet have, takes over. What happens in the last instant is not known. Anyone who tells you with confidence what you would experience there is guessing.

There is a further wrinkle, because real black holes spin. The mathematics for a spinning hole, worked out by Roy Kerr in 1963, contains a second inner horizon, and some of its solutions seem to allow a traveller to avoid the singularity altogether. That has launched many science fiction plots. But in 1990, Eric Poisson and Werner Israel showed that this inner region is likely to be unstable, because any incoming radiation gets amplified without limit as it piles up. Whether the full story saves anyone is contested, and the honest answer is that the problem is still being argued over. For you, the traveller, the safe bet is to assume the centre is final.

Now let us leave the inside and think about what happens to the rest of the universe, because you are about to become part of something that lasts a very long time. You have fallen in. Your mass, about the mass of one person, has been added to the black hole. For a hole as large as Sagittarius A star, that is a change too small to measure. Years pass, then centuries, then millions of years. The black hole sits there, quietly feeding on the occasional passing gas. But in 1974, Stephen Hawking showed that black holes are not perfectly black, and that discovery turns this whole story on its head.

Hawking found that quantum effects near the horizon cause a black hole to give off a faint thermal glow, now called Hawking radiation. The hotter the glow, the smaller the hole. For a black hole with the mass of the Sun, the temperature is about sixty billionths of a kelvin. That is far colder than the cosmic microwave background, which sits at about two point seven kelvin everywhere in space. So right now, every black hole in the universe absorbs more heat than it gives off, and they are all still growing. Only in the far future, when the universe has cooled below their temperature, will they begin to shrink.

How far in the future? For a black hole with the mass of the Sun, evaporation takes about ten to the power of sixty-seven years. The universe today is about thirteen point eight billion years old. So the wait is not a thousand times longer, or a million times longer. It is a number with sixty-seven zeros, set against a number with ten. Larger holes take unimaginably longer still. But eventually, in principle, the hole you fell into would shrink and vanish in a final burst of radiation. And that raises a question that sits at the centre of modern physics. What happens to you when the hole is gone?

This is the black hole information paradox. Quantum mechanics says information can never be destroyed. The full description of everything that fell in must be recoverable in principle. But Hawking's radiation, as he first calculated it, is purely thermal and carries no record of what fell in. If the hole evaporates completely, then everything about you, every atom and its arrangement, appears to vanish from the universe. For decades Hawking argued that information really is lost. In 2004, he conceded that he was probably wrong. But conceding was not the same as finding the answer.

Here is where the field stands. In 2019, calculations by several groups showed that the radiation can, in a precise mathematical sense, carry the information out, following a pattern known as the Page curve. That is a real and important result, but it was done in simplified models, and no one has yet watched the information escape from a realistic black hole. So the likely answer is that you are not destroyed in the deepest sense. Your information may be scrambled across the radiation, returned slowly, in an unreadable code, over a span of time that dwarfs the age of the universe. That is the favoured view. It is not proven.

And now the turn I promised, the one that attacks everything I told you about the horizon. Remember the two accounts of your fall. You, falling, cross without feeling anything. Your friend, watching, sees you freeze and fade at the edge. In the 1990s, Leonard Susskind and others proposed that both can be true, because no single observer ever sees both. This idea is called black hole complementarity. But in 2012, four physicists, Ahmed Almheiri, Donald Marolf, Joseph Polchinski and James Sully, argued that this cannot work, if information is to escape. Their conclusion was shocking. The horizon might not be empty at all.

They called it a firewall. If their argument is right, and if the information really does come out in the radiation, then the smooth, uneventful crossing I described might be false. An infalling traveller might hit a wall of high-energy radiation at the horizon and be destroyed instantly, even for the largest black hole, even where gravity is gentle. Einstein's equivalence principle says that should not happen. Hawking radiation and quantum mechanics together say it might. One of them must give. And this is not settled. Some physicists accept firewalls, some reject them, and some think the paradox is a clue that space itself is built differently from how we imagine.

Think about what that means for the story we just told. The part that seemed safest, the gentle crossing of a huge black hole, is exactly the part under dispute. The moment of no sensation may be the most uncertain moment in the whole timeline. The horizon, which general relativity says is nothing at all, may be the place where relativity and quantum mechanics collide. And every experiment we can run says nothing about it, because the only way to test it is to jump in and never report back. That is the genuinely odd position we are in. The most physically extreme place in the universe is one we can only reason about.

So here is the timeline once more. In the first fractions of a second, near a small black hole, you are stretched into a stream of atoms, thousands of kilometres from the edge. Near Sagittarius A star, you cross the horizon without any sensation, and you have about a minute left, and your best option is to do nothing. Near M87 star, you have about a day. To your friends outside, you fade and freeze at the edge. Over years and centuries, your mass becomes part of the hole. Over a span of time with sixty-seven zeros, the hole may evaporate, and whatever information you were may leak back out into the universe.

And now the question I cannot answer for you, and neither can anyone alive. When you cross the horizon of that giant black hole, do you drift through calmly, as general relativity says, or do you meet a wall of fire, as quantum mechanics may demand?
