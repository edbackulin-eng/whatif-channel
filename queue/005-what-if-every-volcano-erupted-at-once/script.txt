You are standing on a hillside, looking at a mountain that has been quiet your whole life. It is mid-morning. Birds are calling. Then the birds stop.

The ground under your boots begins to hum. It is not a shake yet. It is a low note you feel in your teeth. Your phone buzzes with an alert, then another, and then it goes dead, because every network on Earth is being flooded at once. Far across the ocean, a mountain you have never heard of is doing exactly what yours is about to do. So is every other volcano on the planet.

This cannot happen. Let's say that plainly. Volcanoes are not wired together. Each one is fed by its own pocket of molten rock, with its own plumbing, its own pressure and its own timetable. Nothing known to science could trigger all of them in the same moment. But suppose something did. Suppose the switch is thrown, and every law of physics stays exactly as it is. What happens to you, and to everyone else, over the next few years?

Stay until the end. The strangest part of this story is not the destruction. It is what grows afterwards.

First, we need to know who is in the cast. How many volcanoes is "every volcano"?

The Smithsonian Global Volcanism Program lists roughly thirteen hundred and fifty volcanoes that have erupted since the last ice age ended, about eleven thousand seven hundred years ago. Those are our actors. In a normal year, somewhere between fifty and seventy of them erupt. Most of those are small, and most of them you never hear about.

But that list leaves out the biggest volcanic system on the planet, because it is underwater. The mid-ocean ridges form a seam of cracking rock roughly sixty five thousand kilometres long. It wraps around the globe like the stitching on a baseball. Roughly three quarters of all the magma Earth erupts each year comes out along that seam, in the dark, a few centimetres at a time. So when we say every volcano, we mean the land volcanoes waking up together, while the ocean floor does what it always does, only harder.

Here is the first thing worth knowing about the next sixty seconds. Most of the volcanoes on that list are not monsters. The scenario is not a thousand supervolcanoes. It is a thousand ordinary volcanoes, each erupting at its own typical size. And that is still enough to change the world.

Now, seconds.

In the first few seconds, the shaking arrives. Rising magma cracks rock, and every volcano begins to produce its own swarm of earthquakes. Near the cones the ground heaves and splits. Buildings on the flanks begin to fail. If you are within a few kilometres of a vent, there is nothing you can do in these seconds except hear it. The sound of a big eruption is not a roar. It is a pressure, a thump through your chest that arrives before the noise does.

Then the vents open. Gas expands violently as it reaches the surface, tearing the molten rock into ash and shards of glass. Lava appears at around one thousand degrees Celsius, hot enough to ignite everything it touches. At a lot of volcanoes, it just oozes. At others, the whole mountain tries to leave the ground at once.

And here is the number that makes this scenario different from any disaster in history. The Global Volcano Model project estimates that about eight hundred million people live within one hundred kilometres of a volcano that has erupted since the ice age. That is roughly one person in ten. Hold that thought, because the next stage is the one that kills the most people.

Now, minutes.

Within minutes, the columns reach the sky. In May of 1980, Mount Saint Helens sent an ash column twenty four kilometres high in about fifteen minutes. That was one volcano, in one corner of Washington State. Now picture that repeated around the Pacific Ring of Fire, along the Andes, across Indonesia, Japan, Iceland, the Mediterranean, the East African Rift and the Caribbean.

The worst danger near the vents is not the lava. You can outwalk lava. It is the pyroclastic flow, a glowing avalanche of hot gas and ash that collapses off the eruption column and rushes downhill. The United States Geological Survey says these can reach speeds of up to seven hundred kilometres an hour, and they carry temperatures of hundreds of degrees Celsius. Nobody outruns one. Anyone caught in its path is gone in seconds.

The Saint Helens blast also showed what a sideways explosion can do. It flattened forest across about six hundred square kilometres, an area larger than a major city and its suburbs combined. At Saint Helens the death toll was fifty seven, mostly because the area had been cleared. In this scenario, nobody has had time to clear anything.

Here is what comes next, and it is not the lava, or the ash falling on towns. It is something happening far above your head, in a layer of the atmosphere most people never think about. We will get there. First, the hours.

Now, hours.

By the first hour, the eruption columns are spreading out. Ash falls on cities, sometimes hundreds of kilometres from the vent. A thin dusting is a nuisance. A thick layer is a collapse hazard, because ash is not like snow. It is crushed rock and glass. When it gets wet, it becomes much heavier, and it piles onto roofs until the roofs give way. It also clogs engines, shorts out power lines and coats solar panels. Ash in the water supply turns reservoirs into sludge.

Then comes the sky. In April of 2010, a relatively small eruption of the Icelandic volcano Eyjafjallajökull shut down European airspace. Around one hundred thousand flights were cancelled in eight days, according to Eurocontrol. That was one modest volcano. In our scenario, ash clouds hang over most of the world's busiest air corridors, and every airline pilot who is still in the air faces the same problem. Ash melts inside a jet engine and glazes the turbine, and the engine quits. Every flight lands as fast as it can, wherever it can. After that, the planes stay down for a long time.

Meanwhile, the sea is fighting back too. Volcanic islands slide, and eruptions near the coast displace water. In January of 2022, the Hunga Tonga eruption in the South Pacific blasted a plume to about fifty seven kilometres, higher than any volcanic plume ever measured by modern instruments. It sent a pressure wave around the entire planet, and the wave was recorded on barometers across the world. Think about what that means for a moment. A single underwater volcano rang the atmosphere like a bell. Now picture hundreds of them.

But the real story of this scenario does not unfold in hours. It unfolds in the invisible layer where all of that smoke is headed.

Now, days.

By the second day, the immediate killing is mostly over. The survivors are living in a gray world. Ash lies on the ground and in the air, and the sun looks like a dull coin. Rain falls in places, and the rain is grit. In places where lava met rivers, steam fills the valleys. In others, the first lahars begin, which are volcanic mudflows. When rain mixes with loose ash on steep slopes, it becomes a river of wet concrete. After Pinatubo erupted in the Philippines in 1991, lahars kept killing and burying towns for years, because every typhoon season stirred the deposits up again.

The sulphur is the key. Volcanoes release sulphur dioxide, and when it reaches the stratosphere, it reacts with water to form tiny droplets of sulphuric acid. Those droplets are brilliant mirrors. They reflect sunlight back into space, and they stay up for a long time, because the stratosphere has no rain to wash them out.

Pinatubo is the best measured example we have. It put about twenty million tonnes of sulphur dioxide into the stratosphere, according to the United States Geological Survey. Within about three weeks, the haze had circled the planet. And over the following two years, the whole world cooled by about half a degree Celsius.

Half a degree sounds small. Remember that it was one volcano, and a single one of the many.

So here is the question. How much sulphur do a thousand erupting volcanoes put up? Honestly, no one can give you a single reliable figure, because it depends on how many of them reach the stratosphere, and that depends on latitude and eruption style. Tropical volcanoes loft their sulphur into both hemispheres. High latitude ones tend to stay near home. So let's not pretend we have a precise answer. Let's say instead that the total would be many times Pinatubo, and the cooling would be bigger and last longer. By how much, scientists would argue. We will come back to that argument.

Now, weeks.

Over the first weeks, a veil spreads. The sulphate haze thickens in the stratosphere, and the sky begins to change. Sunsets turn a deep, unnatural red and orange. The daytime sky goes from blue to white. In the weeks after Pinatubo, people on the other side of the planet noticed vivid twilights. In this scenario, those twilights are the beautiful part. The ground temperature is starting to fall.

Crops are the next casualty. Most staple crops are fussy about temperature during a few critical weeks of growth, such as flowering and grain filling. A cool, dim summer reduces yields, and a frost in the wrong week can wipe out a field. The global food system also runs on thin margins. Many countries hold only a few months of grain in storage, and with flights grounded, ports choked and roads buried in ash, even what exists cannot move.

We have a historical model for how this goes, and it is not comforting. In 1815, the Tambora eruption in Indonesia was the largest of the last few centuries. The following year, 1816, became known as the year without a summer. Snow fell in New England in June. Crops failed across Europe and North America, grain prices spiked, and there was hunger and unrest. The global average temperature fell by roughly half a degree, which is a similar figure to Pinatubo, but Tambora was bigger and its effects were felt more strongly in the Northern Hemisphere. In our scenario, that is just the opening act.

And there is a worse historical precedent, from a volcano that did not produce a great explosion at all. Keep that in mind, because it points to something the ash column does not.

Now, the first year.

In 1783, a fissure called Laki split open in Iceland. It did not explode so much as pour. Over about eight months it erupted around fourteen cubic kilometres of lava, and it released vast clouds of sulphur gas near the ground. Livestock died of fluorine poisoning, crops failed, and famine followed. Iceland lost about a fifth of its population. The haze drifted over Europe, and researchers have estimated roughly twenty three thousand extra deaths in Britain alone that summer, from breathing the polluted air.

That matters here because our scenario is not just a stratospheric veil. Down at ground level, every erupting volcano is pumping out sulphur dioxide that turns into acid rain and a choking smog. In the first year, a lot of people are not killed by ash, or lava, or mudflows. They are killed by breathing, and by hunger.

So the timeline of death has three humps. The first is local and instant, near the vents. The second is regional, from ash, collapse, floods and poisoned air. The third is global, and slow, and arrives through the food supply. Careful modelling of volcanic winters, and of other sunlight-blocking scenarios, suggests the third hump is the biggest. But no one has a trustworthy number for it, and anyone who gives you one is guessing.

I want to be careful about one more thing. You may have heard that eruptions of this kind end the world. They almost never do, even the very large ones. About seventy four thousand years ago, Toba in Sumatra erupted, one of the biggest eruptions of the last few hundred thousand years. For a while, scientists proposed that it nearly wiped out our species. Today that idea is heavily disputed. Genetic and archaeological evidence from India and Africa suggests that people there survived Toba with only modest disruption. So the honest answer about Toba is: nobody is certain.

The one event that seems to have come from volcanism on a scale beyond anything we are describing here is much older. About two hundred and fifty two million years ago, the Siberian Traps flooded an area of Russia with lava for what may have been hundreds of thousands of years. That coincides with the end Permian extinction, the worst mass extinction in Earth's history, when the great majority of marine species vanished. The leading explanation links the two. But even there, the mechanism, whether it was the carbon dioxide, the sulphur, the burning of coal beds or something else, is still argued over.

Now, the years.

Let's go into year two and year three. The sulphate veil does not last forever. Droplets slowly grow, and they sink back down into the lower atmosphere. After Pinatubo, the cooling faded in about two years. After a stronger, longer set of eruptions, the haze would linger longer, and scientists would expect a cold stretch of several years. The Northern Hemisphere would see harsh winters, shorter growing seasons and thick sea ice. And the oceans, which store heat, would remember the chill for far longer than the air does.

Then the dust settles, and we face an odd question. What does all this do to carbon dioxide, the gas everyone worries about? You might expect that a thousand erupting volcanoes would cook the planet. They would not, at least not by comparison with us. The United States Geological Survey estimates that human activity releases sixty to one hundred times more carbon dioxide each year than all of the world's volcanoes combined. Even a year of unusually violent volcanic activity adds only a modest bump to the total. In fact, in the short term, the cooling effect of the sulphate veil dominates the warming effect of the gas.

That leads to a strange irony. For a few years, the volcanic veil would hide a fraction of human-caused warming. Then it would fade, and the warming would pick up again, right where it left off.

The water vapour story is less comfortable. When Hunga Tonga erupted, it injected about one hundred and forty six million tonnes of water into the stratosphere, raising the stratosphere's water content by around ten percent, according to satellite measurements by NASA's Aura microwave limb sounder. Water vapour traps heat. Researchers are still debating how much that eruption warmed or cooled the surface, and the estimates differ. So when we ask what a thousand volcanoes would do to the climate over decades, the honest answer is that the first few years are cold, and after that the picture is uncertain.

But I promised you something strange. It is coming now.

So here is the turn.

In August of 2008, a small volcano called Kasatochi, on a remote island in the Aleutians, erupted. It was not a major eruption. It threw ash across a wide stretch of the northeast Pacific Ocean. And in the following two years, something odd happened a long way away. In 2010, more sockeye salmon came back to the Fraser River in Canada than in almost a century. The run was many times larger than forecasters had predicted. Tens of millions of fish.

A team led by Canadian oceanographers proposed an explanation. Volcanic ash is rich in iron. Much of the open North Pacific is short of iron, which is the bottleneck for the tiny algae at the bottom of the food chain. When Kasatochi's ash settled on the water, it may have triggered a vast bloom of plankton, which fed the young salmon that were heading out to sea at just the right moment. That link is still debated. Other researchers point to ocean conditions that year, and some say the evidence is suggestive but not conclusive. But the mechanism itself, iron from volcanic ash fertilising the sea, is real and has been observed.

Now scale that up. A thousand volcanoes would drop ash on far more of the ocean than one small island in the Aleutians could. Surface waters across large areas would get a pulse of iron, silica and other nutrients. And on land, there is a parallel story. Volcanic ash weathers into some of the most fertile soil on Earth. It is the reason so many people have always lived beside volcanoes, from the slopes of Java to the vineyards of southern Italy. They accept the risk, because the land feeds them.

So the tail of this disaster has a green edge. The first year is a catastrophe of cold, hunger and poison. But the ocean may bloom, and the ash-covered fields, once the acid is washed out, may be remarkably productive. Plankton that bloom take carbon dioxide out of the air, which is a small cooling nudge to the climate on top of the sulphate veil. The planet, in its own indifferent way, starts to repair itself with the same material that broke it.

I want to be careful about how far to push that. A bloom that feeds salmon is not a bloom that feeds eight billion people. The recovery takes years, and in those years, what matters is whether anyone remains to farm the new soil.

That brings us to the people.

Think about who survives. People who live far from a volcano, in places like the interior of Australia, southern Africa, or the Great Plains, would lose their power and probably their trade, but not their land. Those who live in the shadow of a volcano, and who are not killed outright, would face a world where the sky stays dim and the harvest fails. The survivors would probably be those with stored food, local farming and a community willing to share.

And the survivors have a map. Humanity has already lived through the year without a summer. We have satellites that can track a sulphate veil and models that can forecast a cold spell years ahead. We have seed banks and grain reserves, and some countries are beginning to plan for abrupt sunlight reduction. Whether we would use that knowledge in time, with the world in chaos, is a question for you.

Let me bring the timeline together.

In seconds, the ground shakes and the vents open. In minutes, columns of ash climb to tens of kilometres, and pyroclastic flows kill everything near the volcanoes. In hours, ash falls on cities and the world's skies close to aircraft. In days, mudflows and acid rain spread, and the sulphur starts its climb into the stratosphere. In weeks, a veil of sulphate droplets circles the planet and the sun dims. In the first year, crops fail and the toxic haze takes a second toll. And over the years, the world cools, then recovers, while ash feeds oceans and soils that will be unusually rich.

The one thing it does not do is end the Earth. The planet has seen far worse, and it recovers each time. Volcanoes are not the enemy of the planet. They built the atmosphere, the oceans and the soil we stand on. The same restless interior that could ruin a few years of human history is the reason there is a human history at all.

So here is the question I want to leave with you. Suppose a warning came, and you had one year to prepare for this. Would you move away from the volcanoes, or toward the places the ash would make fertile? And what would you stockpile first?
