You are halfway through a sentence when it happens. Your phone buzzes once and goes quiet. The bars at the top of the screen are still full, but the page you were loading never finishes. The map spins its little wheel. Your video call freezes on a stranger's open mouth. Nothing has exploded. Nothing has crashed. Every link that joins one computer to another has simply gone silent, everywhere on Earth, in the same instant.

Before we go any further, a plain statement. This cannot happen. The internet is not one machine with one switch. It is tens of thousands of separate networks, owned by phone companies, universities, governments and corporations, that have agreed to pass each other's traffic. Nothing short of a planetary catastrophe would silence all of them together. But we are going to pretend it did. We will keep every law of physics switched on and watch what follows. The cables stay in the ground. The computers stay on their shelves. They just stop talking to each other.

Here is the part that should worry you. The most important piece of the whole system is one you have never seen, and you have never once used on purpose. When it fails, even the parts that survived become useless. We will get to it in a few minutes. First, let me show you how big this is.

According to the International Telecommunication Union, about five and a half billion people were using the internet in 2024. That is roughly two thirds of humanity. The other two point six billion or so were offline. We will come back to them, because their story in this scenario is stranger than you might guess.

The people who are online mostly do not reach each other through the sky. Satellites get the headlines, but about ninety-nine percent of the data that crosses between continents travels through cables on the sea floor. There are more than five hundred of them in service. When you watch a video filmed on another continent, it arrives as pulses of light that have crossed an ocean floor in the dark.

So let us start the clock.

One second after the silence. Everything that was in flight is lost. Light in optical fibre travels at about two thirds of its speed in a vacuum, which is roughly two hundred thousand kilometres every second. That is fast, but it is not instant. A pulse going from one side of the planet to the other covers about twenty thousand kilometres, and even in perfect conditions that takes around a tenth of a second. Whatever light is already inside the glass keeps travelling and arrives at the far end. But there is nothing there to read it. The routers that would have passed it on have stopped passing anything. Engineers call this packet loss. Right now it is total.

Ten seconds. Your phone is not dead. It is a very capable computer that has just lost its window to the world. The camera works. The clock works. Anything stored on the device works. And one thing works that might surprise you. Your location dot keeps moving. The satellites of the Global Positioning System orbit more than twenty thousand kilometres above you, and they only broadcast. They never listen. They have no idea whether the internet exists. Your phone hears them, calculates where it is, and carries on, although the map underneath the dot may slowly go blank as the pieces it had saved run out.

Thirty seconds. The video you were watching stops. A video buffer holds only a short stretch of what is coming, so the last few seconds play out and then freeze. In millions of homes, people do what people always do. They restart the router. It does not help, because the router is fine. Everything past it is gone.

Now, your phone calls. This depends on where you live and who your carrier is, and anyone who gives you one confident answer is guessing. Many mobile networks now carry voice as data, and the towers are connected back to the core of the network by fibre. Some towers may still talk to the phones near them, but the thing they need to reach is not there. A few old copper landlines might ring. Those are rarer every year. For most people, the first honest sign that something enormous has happened is that they cannot call anyone to ask.

Stay with me. In the next stretch, I will show you what the shops are about to find out, and then a real event from 2021 that explains how long a repair like this might take. It is not what you expect.

One minute. Here is the thing I promised. Computers find each other by number, not by name. The system that turns a name into a number is the Domain Name System, and it is the phone book of the internet. At the very top of it sit thirteen root server addresses, run by twelve different organisations, and copied onto well over a thousand machines around the world. Nearly every lookup you have ever done started with one of those, or with a saved copy held by your internet provider. Those saved copies expire after a time set by whoever owns the name. For some it is minutes. For others it is a day.

Here is the cruel part. Even if some network island survives, say a university campus or a company's own network, the names stop working as the saved copies expire, and a name that does not resolve is a place that does not exist. The cables could be perfect and the phone book would still be blank. So the damage does not only come from what is broken. It comes from what can no longer be found.

Five minutes. Walk into a shop. The till wants to ask your bank if your card is good. It cannot ask. Some terminals have an offline mode that approves small purchases up to a limit, and the bank sorts it out later. But that is a design choice, not a law of nature, and it varies by shop, by card, and by country. Cash, the thing a lot of people stopped carrying, becomes the only money everyone fully trusts. And a cash machine needs a line to your bank to know your balance, so the cash you can get is the cash already in your pocket.

Ten minutes. Think about the money itself. Your savings are not coins in a vault. They are entries in databases. Those entries are still there. Every bank's ledger is intact, in a building, on a disk, humming away. But the ledgers cannot agree with each other. A payment sent a minute before the silence may have left one bank and never reached the other. Somewhere, a number is missing, and nobody can tell which side of the gap it fell on. Sorting that out is slow, human, and done with paper and phone lines, if there are phone lines.

Thirty minutes. In the sky, the planes that are already flying keep flying. Air traffic control runs mostly on radar, radio and dedicated links, not on the public internet. Pilots can talk to controllers and controllers can see the aircraft. But the systems that plan routes, load fuel, schedule crews, book gates and tell a hold in another country that you are coming lean heavily on networks. Expect planes to land safely, and expect most of them to stay on the ground afterwards.

Now I want to take you to a Monday in October, because a real event shows what is about to go wrong with the repair. Hold that thought.

On the fourth of October 2021, Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp disappeared from the internet for about six hours. This was not an attack. A routine maintenance command went wrong, and the company's networks stopped telling the rest of the internet where its servers were. Facebook's own engineering write-up explained the trap. The tools its engineers normally used to respond to a problem like this were affected by the same fault. And getting into the data centres to fix things by hand took extra time, because those buildings are built to be hard to get into.

Read that again. The people with the knowledge could not reach the machines, because the machines were the way they reached anything. This is the lesson of the whole scenario. Our world does not only run on the internet. The repair of the internet runs on the internet. Every company has chat tools that run in the cloud, tickets that run in the cloud, and logins that check with a server far away. One hour in, for almost every organisation on Earth, the problem is not broken hardware. It is locked doors and missing passwords.

Meanwhile the data centres hum. Those buildings used roughly four hundred and fifteen terawatt hours of electricity in 2024, according to the International Energy Agency, about one and a half percent of the world's electricity. Servers do not know they are alone. A server sitting idle still draws a substantial share of the power it draws when busy, so for a while the world burns enormous amounts of electricity to run machines that nobody can talk to.

Three hours. Hospitals are the first place where this stops being an inconvenience. Most keep written downtime procedures for exactly this kind of day, and staff practise them. Doctors can still treat the person in front of them. But lab results, scans, drug records and allergy lists often live in systems that sit somewhere else. The harm is not dramatic. It is slow, quiet, and made of small unknowns: a dose that cannot be checked, a result that cannot be found.

Here is the next hook, and it is about food. How long does a city have before the shelves begin to empty? The honest answer is contested, and I will tell you where the argument lies.

Twelve hours. Night falls. The lights stay on, and that is worth understanding. Power stations and water plants are deliberately kept apart from the public internet, at least in their core controls, and they have manual procedures. This is one reason the scenario does not become an instant blackout. But grid operators trade power across regions, predict demand using weather feeds, and coordinate with other operators through connected systems. Those people have to switch to phones, radios and experience. Engineers disagree about how long that holds. Some say days. Others say a good deal longer. Nobody has really tested it.

One day. Roughly eighty percent of world trade by volume moves by sea, according to the United Nations Conference on Trade and Development. A container ship can still steer by satellite navigation and radio. What it cannot do is learn which port has room for it, which customs forms were approved, or which trucks are waiting. Ports and rail yards are managed by software that talks to other software. Without it, cranes sit still and containers pile up. Everything on the planet that was supposed to arrive tomorrow is now arriving at some other date.

And that brings us to the shelves. Modern shops are built to hold as little stock as they can, because stock costs money. Orders are placed by software, trucks are scheduled by software, and the loop is tight. The exact number of days a supermarket can last varies by product, by country and by season, so anyone quoting a single figure is simplifying. But for fresh food, it is days, not weeks. By the third day, in the places that depend on that tight loop, the shelves look strange.

Week one. What do you do? People do what they have always done. They walk. They gather in squares. They read paper notices. Local radio becomes the most valuable station on the dial. Amateur radio operators, who spend decades practising for exactly this, begin passing messages across the country and across oceans without any cables at all. The slowest form of networking, carrying a drive in your hand, comes back too. Engineers call it sneakernet. A famous line from the computer scientist Andrew Tanenbaum says never to underestimate the bandwidth of a station wagon full of tapes. It is huge bandwidth with terrible delay, and for a few weeks it is a serious option.

Now the question everyone asks. How long until it comes back? Two real events give us a hint, with a warning. In January 2022, an undersea volcanic eruption near Tonga broke the country's only submarine cable. Tonga stayed largely cut off from the internet for about five weeks, until a specialised repair ship could reach the damage and splice the cable. In 2011, the Egyptian government ordered the country's connections shut for about five days, and they came back by flipping settings back, not by laying new glass.

Those two cases bracket our problem. Our scenario is not a broken cable. It is every connection, on every continent. The world has only a small fleet of cable repair ships, and they take weeks to reach a fault. But in our scenario the physical glass is mostly intact. So the work is not laying cable. It is reconfiguring millions of routers, one site at a time, with technicians plugging into the front of each machine, because remote access is gone. That is why the real answer sits somewhere between days and months, and people who know the field give different answers.

One month. The first sections to return are not what you would guess. They are small, boring and old. Email between organisations that agree to talk. File transfers. Text messages. Plain, thin connections running on equipment that is already in place. Video calls come much later. The first thing restored is the least pretty part of the internet.

I promised you a surprise, and it is coming. It has to do with two and a half billion people, and with something that already happened to the web while you were not looking.

First, the strange story of those who were offline all along. For about two point six billion people, nothing changes today. No phone buzzes and stops. But their lives are tied to the system in ways they do not see: the price of grain, the fertiliser on the field, the medicine in the clinic, the loan, the remittance from a relative abroad. They feel it in the supply chains first, not in their pockets. Being offline did not protect them from the silence. It only meant they never saw it coming.

Now the first surprise, and it is a gentle one. The thing you are afraid of losing is surprisingly small. The whole of Wikipedia in text fits on a cheap memory card, and there are volunteers who already hand it out to places with no connection. A library of human knowledge is not the problem. The problem is what sits between the library and you.

Year one. The economy has taken a blow whose size nobody can honestly state, because nobody has measured a world without it. What we do have are small experiments. In a well-known study published in the American Economic Review in 2020, economists paid more than two thousand people to deactivate Facebook for four weeks in the run-up to the 2018 American midterm elections. Those people spent more time with friends and family, and reported small improvements in their wellbeing. The effect was modest, and researchers argue about how far it generalises. It is not proof that a world without the internet is happier. It is a reminder that the loss is not only a loss.

Now I want to give you the turn I promised, and I think it is the most important idea in this video.

Think about where the internet came from. In October 1969, a computer at the University of California, Los Angeles tried to send the word login to a computer at the Stanford Research Institute. The system crashed after two letters. The first message ever sent on the ARPANET was, by accident, the word lo. By December of that year the network had four computers. Four. On the first of January 1983, the ARPANET switched over to the protocols we still use today. The whole thing grew from a handful of machines and a set of rules that are public, written down, and short enough that a small team can understand them.

That is the surprise. The internet is not mainly a pile of hardware. It is an agreement, and an agreement can be rebuilt. If every cable vanished, we would have to lay new ones, and that would take years. But if only the connections went dark, the rules that make the network work are written in documents that any engineer can read. The first links would be crude, and they would join up, because joining up is what the rules were designed for.

And a related myth deserves a correction, with a warning that historians disagree. You may have heard that the internet was built to survive a nuclear war. The truth is more tangled. In the early 1960s, the engineer Paul Baran at the RAND Corporation did study how to build communications that could survive an attack, and his ideas on splitting messages into pieces fed into later thinking. But many of the people who built the ARPANET itself have said their goal was sharing expensive computers between research sites, not surviving a war. Both stories have supporters. What nobody disputes is the design that came out: no centre, many routes, and a habit of going around damage.

Here is the second turn, and it may be the one that stays with you. Even if we never turn the internet off, it is already disappearing, slowly, from the inside. In 2024, the Pew Research Center looked at a large sample of webpages and found that thirty-eight percent of the pages that existed in 2013 could no longer be reached by 2023. Links break. Sites close. Companies shut down. Pages are rewritten. Researchers call it link rot. A great deal of what people wrote, argued over and shared a decade ago has already gone, and nobody held a funeral.

So the scenario in this video is not a strange new kind of loss. It is the loss that happens every day, sped up, made visible, and happening all at once. The part of the internet that would really vanish for good is not the cables, and it is not the protocols. It is the content nobody kept a second copy of. The organisations that save the web, such as the Internet Archive, are a rescue operation, not a guarantee, and they hold only what they happened to catch.

Years on, the shape of the world is different. Maybe a small, careful network returns, built by people who remember how fragile it was. Maybe paper makes a comeback in places where trust has to be physical. Maybe some of the habits of that first quiet month survive: neighbours who know each other, notice boards, a slower rhythm. And maybe the opposite, a rush to rebuild every convenience exactly as before. The science cannot tell you which. That part is a choice.

Here is what we do know. The internet is among the most complex things humans have ever made, and for one hour in 2021 a single mistake at one company showed how the repair can be trapped inside the thing that broke. Add in the money, the ships, the shelves and the hospitals, and you see a world that has quietly tied itself together with glass. It is also a world that, in a few remarkable decades, learned to build that glass out of nothing but a few computers and a set of rules.

So here is my question, and I really want your answers. If the internet vanished tomorrow morning and you had one day to prepare, what is the first thing you would do, and what is the one thing you would be quietly glad to be rid of? Tell me in the comments, and tell me why.
