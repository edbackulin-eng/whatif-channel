Picture a clear, cold night. You are standing in a dark field, looking up at one of the brightest lights in the sky. It does not twinkle. It shines steady and pale gold. It is not a star. It is Jupiter, and it is about to disappear.

You do not know that yet. Nothing in the sky looks wrong. But the real event has already happened, and the light reaching your eyes is old news. In a few minutes, you will watch the largest planet in the solar system go dark. No flash. No flicker. No sound. Just a patch of empty black where a world used to be.

Stay with me. The first thing that goes wrong is not what you expect, and the last thing that goes wrong is something almost nobody talks about.

Now the honest part. Jupiter cannot vanish. Nothing we know of can delete a planet. Jupiter has about three hundred and eighteen times the mass of Earth, and mass does not simply stop existing. Conservation of energy and momentum forbid it. If you converted all that mass into energy, using Einstein's famous equation, you would release about as much energy as the Sun puts out in fourteen billion years. That is not what we are doing here. In this video, Jupiter is quietly removed, with nothing released and nothing left behind. Everything else obeys the normal rules. Gravity, orbits, light, and time all behave exactly as they should.

To feel what we are deleting, start with its size. Jupiter is about eleven times wider than Earth. It has more than two and a half times the mass of all the other planets put together. Its Great Red Spot, a storm astronomers have watched for generations, is wider than our entire planet. And yet next to the Sun, Jupiter is a lightweight. It has only about one thousandth of the Sun's mass. Keep that number in your pocket. It is the reason Earth does not fall apart in this story. At least, not straight away.

Now the clock starts. In the first second, the only place where anything has changed is Jupiter itself. It orbits about seven hundred and seventy eight million kilometres from the Sun, which is five point two times farther out than Earth. Its gravity is gone. Its magnetic field is gone. The sunlight that was about to strike its clouds simply keeps travelling into the dark.

On Earth, nothing happens. And that is the first surprise. Gravity is not instant. Changes in gravity travel at the speed of light. We know this from a measurement made in 2017, when detectors caught gravitational waves from two neutron stars colliding about a hundred and thirty million light years away. Telescopes then saw a burst of gamma rays about one point seven seconds later. After a journey of a hundred and thirty million years, gravity and light arrived within two seconds of each other. So no news of Jupiter's disappearance can reach you faster than light.

How long does the news take? The distance between Earth and Jupiter is never the same twice. At its closest, Jupiter is about five hundred and eighty eight million kilometres away. At its farthest, about nine hundred and sixty eight million. Light needs between roughly thirty three and fifty four minutes to cross that gap. For that long, your sky and your planet carry on as if nothing has happened. Earth keeps moving at about thirty kilometres per second, along an orbit still shaped by the pull of a planet that no longer exists.

How big was that pull? Even at its closest, Jupiter tugs on Earth roughly sixteen thousand times more weakly than the Sun does. You would never feel it. Which raises a puzzle. If Earth barely feels Jupiter, why do scientists argue about whether Jupiter protects us or endangers us? We will get to that. First, you need to see what happens to Jupiter's family.

Now the minutes. Say you are watching Jupiter through a telescope. For somewhere between thirty three and fifty four minutes, it stays right where it was. Then the last light that left the planet arrives, and the planet is not there. At the same instant, the last of its gravity reaches us. Both messages arrive together, because both travel at the same speed.

Look closer and you notice something strange. Jupiter is gone, but its moons are not. Io, Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto are separate worlds. Ganymede is the largest moon in the solar system, and it is bigger than the planet Mercury. They still catch sunlight. They are still there. But they no longer have anything to orbit.

Here is what that means. Orbiting is just falling around something, over and over, without ever hitting it. Take away the thing, and the falling stops. A moon simply flies off in a straight line at the speed it had. Io was moving at about seventeen kilometres per second. In a single day, it covers about one and a half million kilometres.

But there is a twist inside that twist. Those moons were also travelling with Jupiter around the Sun, at about thirteen kilometres per second. Add the two motions together and each moon gets a brand new path. To escape the Sun's gravity from Jupiter's distance, you need about eighteen and a half kilometres per second. A moon that happens to be moving forward along Jupiter's track at that moment gets a boost. Io, in the best case, would be moving at around thirty kilometres per second. That is far beyond escape speed. That moon is leaving the solar system for good. A moon that happens to be moving backward gets the opposite. Ganymede, at about ten point nine kilometres per second, could be left with just over two kilometres per second around the Sun. It would begin to fall inward on a long, plunging orbit toward the Sun. Which moon does what depends entirely on where it was in its orbit at that instant. A family of worlds, scattered by luck.

Those moons are the dramatic part. The next part is quieter, and it involves thousands of rocks you have probably never heard of.

Now the hours. Jupiter does not travel alone. Sixty degrees ahead of it in its orbit, and sixty degrees behind, sit two great swarms of asteroids called the Trojans. More than ten thousand are known, and there are certainly more. They sit there because the pull of the Sun and the pull of Jupiter combine to make two stable pockets in space. Take Jupiter away and the pockets disappear. Nothing visible happens in the first hours. The rocks keep moving the same way at the same speed. But they are no longer held in place. They are just asteroids on ordinary orbits, and over the coming centuries they drift away from their old positions and spread around the orbit. NASA's Lucy spacecraft, launched in 2021, is heading for them now, because they may be leftover building blocks from the birth of the planets. Lucy would still find them. It just would not find them tidy.

And Lucy is not the only spacecraft with a problem. Europa Clipper launched in October 2024 and is due at Jupiter in 2030. Europe's Juice mission launched in April 2023 and is due in 2031. Both are aimed at a place that would now hold nothing. Even a mission that finished long ago owes Jupiter a debt. In 2007, the New Horizons spacecraft swung past Jupiter and took a gravity boost that shaved about three years off its trip to Pluto. Without Jupiter, that shortcut would never have existed.

But the strangest thing we lose in these first days is not on any spacecraft. It is in the Sun itself. Hold that thought.

Now the days. We say that planets orbit the Sun. Really, the Sun and its planets orbit a shared balance point. For the Sun and Jupiter alone, that balance point sits just outside the Sun's surface, about one point oh seven solar radii from its centre. So every twelve years or so, the Sun traces a loop around a point in empty space. Take Jupiter away, and that balance point drops back inside the Sun. Saturn, now the heaviest planet left, would by itself put it only about sixty percent of the way from the Sun's centre to its surface. The Sun stops swinging out and settles down.

The night sky changes in a smaller, more human way. Jupiter is usually outshone only by the Sun, the Moon and Venus. Babylonian astronomers were tracking its path across the sky more than two thousand years ago. In a few days, the evening sky is simply a little emptier, and the old calendar of Jupiter's slow walk through the constellations has nothing left to follow.

There is also a loss you could never see. Jupiter's magnetic field is a natural particle accelerator. It flings electrons out into space, and some of them reach Earth. Spacecraft near our planet detect them, and their numbers rise and fall roughly every thirteen months, which is how long it takes Earth and Jupiter to line up again. Without Jupiter, that faint stream stops. It does nothing to you. But it is one of the odder fingerprints Jupiter leaves on our neighbourhood, and it is gone.

So far, Earth has barely noticed. Here is the part where it starts to notice, and it takes much longer than you would think.

Now the years. In the first year after Jupiter vanishes, the planets keep their paths almost perfectly. The Sun holds them, and Jupiter was only a small adjustment. But it was an adjustment, and adjustments add up. Take Mercury. Its orbit slowly turns, because of the tug of the other planets. Jupiter's share of that turning is about one hundred and fifty arcseconds per century, second only to Venus. That sounds small, and it is. But it is exactly the sort of tiny angle that helped prove general relativity. The relativistic part of Mercury's turning is only about forty three arcseconds per century. Jupiter's tug is more than three times bigger, and astronomers had to account for it before they could see Einstein's contribution at all.

Now look outward, to the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter. All of it together weighs less than our Moon. In 1866, an astronomer named Daniel Kirkwood noticed that it has gaps, orbits where almost no asteroids are found. The gaps sit at distances where an asteroid would circle the Sun a whole number of times for each orbit of Jupiter. At the three to one gap, about two and a half times Earth's distance from the Sun, an asteroid would go around three times for every Jupiter orbit. It meets Jupiter in the same place again and again. Each meeting gives it the same small nudge, until its orbit is stretched and it is thrown out of the belt. Without Jupiter, the nudges stop. The gaps stop being carved.

That matters for us. Many of the asteroids that come near Earth were delivered by exactly those resonances. A study led by William Bottke found that the three to one gap is one of the main routes into the inner solar system. Cut that route, and the supply of new near-Earth asteroids changes. Each one already here lasts only a few million years before it hits a planet, falls into the Sun, or is thrown out. So the change would be slow. Not decades. Millions of years.

Then there are the comets. In July 1994, a comet called Shoemaker Levy 9, which Jupiter had torn into more than twenty pieces, slammed into the planet over six days. The scars were visible in modest backyard telescopes. Since 2010, amateur astronomers have caught several more flashes from smaller impacts. Jupiter is a magnet for loose material, and that is where the idea of Jupiter as a shield comes from.

That idea sounds simple. It is not. And the real answer turns the story upside down. But before that, let's zoom out to the long, slow scale.

Now the centuries. Jupiter and Saturn are locked in a slow beat. Five Jupiter orbits take almost exactly as long as two Saturn orbits. So the two giants return to nearly the same positions again and again, and each time they pull on each other a little. The beat repeats about every nine hundred years. Astronomers noticed it long before they understood it, and it puzzled Laplace in the late eighteenth century. Remove Jupiter and Saturn loses its oldest partner.

Earth has its own slow rhythm. Our orbit is not a perfect circle. Right now it is off circular by about one point seven percent. Over hundreds of thousands of years that number rises and falls, from almost zero to about six percent. One strong rhythm in that cycle repeats about every four hundred and five thousand years, and it comes from Venus and Jupiter tugging on each other and on Earth. These rhythms help pace the ice ages. Without Jupiter, that particular rhythm would change or vanish. How much that matters for climate is not settled. Earth's tilt, which matters more for seasons, is steadied mainly by the Moon, and the Moon is not going anywhere.

There is a deeper problem, though. The inner solar system is chaotic. In 1989, the French astronomer Jacques Laskar showed that small errors in planetary positions multiply by about ten every ten million years. So nobody can say exactly where Earth will be in a hundred million years. Whether a solar system without Jupiter would be calmer or wilder is something researchers can only test by running simulations, and the answers depend on the assumptions. Anyone who tells you Earth would be flung out of its orbit, or that nothing would change, is claiming more than the science supports.

Now to the idea everyone has heard. Jupiter, the cosmic bodyguard, soaking up comets and saving Earth. In 1994, the planetary scientist George Wetherill published calculations suggesting that a solar system without a Jupiter would see far more comet impacts on Earth. The idea spread, and it turned into a fact in documentaries. Then Jonti Horner and Barrie Jones tested it with more detailed simulations. They found that the answer depends on what is coming at you. For long period comets, Jupiter does help. For asteroids, some of their simulations suggest Earth would be hit less often without Jupiter, because Jupiter's resonances are what throw asteroids inward in the first place. For the icy bodies in between, the picture is mixed. Scientists still argue about the overall balance. So if you came here expecting to hear that Earth is doomed without its bodyguard, the evidence does not say that. It does not say the opposite, either.

And now the turn I promised. Everything so far assumes that our solar system, with its Jupiter, is the normal case. Is it? Astronomers have spent three decades counting. The first planet found around a Sun-like star was 51 Pegasi b, in 1995, by Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz. It is a giant that orbits its star every four days or so, baked by its sun. Giants like our Jupiter, on cold, wide orbits, turned out to be much harder to find, and rarer. Surveys put Jupiter-like planets on orbits like ours at roughly six to fourteen percent of Sun-like stars. The exact figure is still argued over. But whichever number you choose, most stars do not have a Jupiter.

Meanwhile, the Kepler telescope found that about one in four Sun-like stars hosts a planet one to two times Earth's width on a close orbit. So the version of the solar system where Jupiter never existed is not exotic. It may well be the common kind. We simply do not know how often such systems end up with a habitable world, because we have only studied one planet with life on it. Everything we say about the effect of Jupiter on life rests on a sample of one.

There is one last thing, and it brings us back to the Sun's wobble. When astronomers look for planets around other stars, one method is to watch the star move. The Sun's motion around that balance point, caused by Jupiter, is about twelve and a half metres per second. Saturn gives about two point eight. Earth gives about nine centimetres per second. If an alien astronomer watched our Sun with a spectrograph as good as the best we have, Jupiter would be the loud announcement that says this star has planets. Earth would barely whisper. Delete Jupiter, and the loudest signal drops to a few metres per second. So in one strange sense, Jupiter is not Earth's bodyguard. It is our loudest advertisement. Whether anyone is listening, we cannot say.
