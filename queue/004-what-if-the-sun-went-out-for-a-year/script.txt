You are standing in a park at noon. The sun is warm on the back of your neck. Your shadow lies short and sharp on the grass, and somewhere behind you a dog is barking at nothing. Then the sun goes out. There is no flash and no sound. For the next eight minutes and twenty seconds, you notice nothing at all. You finish your sentence. You take a sip of coffee. Your shadow stays exactly where it is. Then the light that was already on its way runs out, and the middle of the day turns into the darkest night you have ever seen. Stay with me, because the first thing to go wrong is not the cold. And the thing that saves a few of us is not what you would guess.

Before we go any further, a plain statement. This cannot happen. The sun is not a lamp with a switch. It is a ball of hot plasma about one point three nine million kilometres across, and it holds nearly all the mass in the solar system. It shines because fusion in its core turns hydrogen into helium, and it has been doing that for about four point six billion years. Nothing we know of could stop that in an instant, and nothing could restart it a year later. So this video cheats, and I will tell you exactly how. We keep the sun's gravity, because without it the planets would fly off in straight lines and the story would be over in minutes. We remove only the light and the heat. Everything else, we leave the physics switched on.

There is a second reason this is a cheat, and it is a good one. Even if the core really did stop fusing today, you would not see the sun change for a very long time. Energy made in the core does not travel straight out. It is absorbed and re-emitted again and again, wandering through plasma so dense that escape takes ages. Estimates for how long a packet of energy needs to reach the surface run from around ten thousand years to a couple of hundred thousand. Researchers disagree on the exact figure, because it depends on how you model the opacity of the sun's interior. But every estimate agrees on the headline. The sunlight on your neck today was made before the first cities were built. So in our scenario, someone has blocked the light at the very last step and left the furnace behind it burning. Keep that in mind. It comes back at the end, and it changes how this story finishes.

Now the timeline, and we start with seconds. The answer is that nothing happens. The sun sits about one hundred and forty-nine point six million kilometres away, and light covers that distance in roughly eight minutes and twenty seconds. No news can outrun light. So for more than eight minutes, every plant, every instrument and every eye on Earth is still being fed by a sun that has already stopped shining. Gravity obeys the same speed limit. Physicists confirmed it most clearly in 2017, when a gravitational wave and a burst of gamma rays from two colliding neutron stars arrived about one point seven seconds apart, after a journey of roughly one hundred and thirty million years. In our version, gravity never changes at all. So the Earth keeps falling around the sun at about twenty-nine point eight kilometres every second, exactly as before.

In the next few minutes, you lose the one thing you did not know you were using to see everything else.

At eight minutes and twenty seconds, the last light arrives. It does not fade like a sunset. It stops. The blue sky is the first casualty, because the blue sky is nothing but sunlight bouncing off air. With no sunlight there is nothing to bounce, and within moments the sky is black, with stars in it, at noon. Then you notice the Moon is gone. Moonlight is only sunlight, reflected from a surface that returns about twelve percent of what hits it. The Moon is still up there, still orbiting, but it is a dark rock now. Venus, Jupiter and Mars vanish for the same reason. What remains is the stars, which are suns shining on their own, and the faint band of the Milky Way. And somewhere in that sky is a hole. The sun is about half a degree wide, almost exactly the size of the full Moon, and it blots out the stars behind it. You could find it only by what it hides.

An hour in, the park is quiet. Your breath is starting to fog. The streetlights are still on, because the grid has not yet noticed anything, but above the glow of the city there are more stars than you have ever seen from here. Everyone around you is looking up. Nobody is panicking yet. They think it is an eclipse, or a power failure, or a prank. They are about to find out which of those it is not.

Here is the number that drives everything that follows. At the top of the atmosphere, sunlight delivers about thirteen hundred and sixty-one watts for every square metre facing the sun. Average that over the whole planet, day and night, poles and tropics, and the figure is about three hundred and forty watts per square metre. Switch that off, and the Earth keeps radiating heat into space at roughly two hundred and forty watts per square metre. Income drops to zero. Spending barely changes. The planet is a bank account with one huge withdrawal and no more deposits. How fast the balance falls decides who lives. And the answer begins with a surprise about the air.

The whole atmosphere holds about as much heat as the top few metres of the ocean. That is all. So the air cools first, and fastest. Over the first day, the places that were in daylight when the sun died lose their warmth as though night had come and refused to leave. Deserts, which already swing hard between day and night, fall quickly. Humid coasts hold on longer, because water is a slow store of heat. By the end of the first day you are wearing every coat you own. By the end of the first week, the coat is not the problem. The problem is the weather itself. Wind exists because the sun heats the planet unevenly. Rain exists because the sun lifts water out of the oceans. With no heating, the great winds begin to die, evaporation stops, and the clouds drift away and are not replaced. A clear, still, dry sky is one of the most efficient ways there is to lose heat. So the cooling speeds up.

But the first thing to actually start killing things is not the cold at all.

It is the food. Photosynthesis stops the moment the light does. In the ocean, the microscopic algae at the base of nearly every marine food chain live for only days to weeks, and they carry out a huge share of all the photosynthesis on Earth, perhaps half. When they stop, the surface ocean's food web has no income and starts spending its savings. On land, plants can live on stored sugars for a while, but they are not built to freeze. And you will not suffocate, which many people assume. The air is about twenty-one percent oxygen, and the dark does not take it away. Every plant and every plankton cell on Earth could die and rot or burn, and it would use only a small fraction of that oxygen. The air stays breathable for far longer than anyone will be alive to need it.

Stored food buys time, but not much. Global grain stocks cover a few months of use, according to the Food and Agriculture Organization's regular supply and demand briefs, and they sit in warehouses that nobody can heat. Greenhouses need light, which now means electric lamps, which means fuel, in a world where the same fuel is needed to keep people from freezing. Every choice has a price, and every price is paid in energy.

So let us do the arithmetic on energy, because it is brutal. Humanity uses about twenty terawatts, all sources combined. The Earth intercepts about one hundred and seventy-four thousand terawatts of sunlight. Burn every fuel we can reach, and we would replace well under a tenth of one percent of what we lost. Solar panels produce nothing, of course. Wind turbines slow with the dying winds. Hydroelectric dams depend on rain, and rain depends on evaporation. What is left is fossil fuel, which supplies about eighty percent of the world's primary energy, and nuclear power, which makes about nine percent of the world's electricity from fuel that is loaded into reactors for roughly eighteen to twenty-four months at a time. Those can run for a while. But they can heat a few rooms, not a continent.

There is one place on Earth where people have already practised something like this. At the South Pole, the sun sets around March and does not rise again for roughly six months. The scientists who stay through that winter live in heated buildings, with food and fuel delivered before the last aircraft leaves. They get through the dark without much trouble. But they are not a model for the rest of us, and the reason is the gap between dark and dead. They have supply. They have a summer before the winter, and a summer after it. In our scenario, nobody is coming to resupply anyone.

Another place has an answer of its own. In Iceland, about ninety percent of homes are already heated with hot water from the ground. That water is warmed by the planet, not the sun, and it keeps flowing whether the sky is lit or not. Places like that, along with deep mines and anywhere near a working reactor, are where the survivors would gather. How many survive is a guess, and I will not insult you with a number I cannot support. What I can say is that it is a small fraction, and that nearly all of them are underground, or beside something hot.

And now we have to talk about how cold this really gets.

The coldest air temperature ever measured at the surface of the Earth was minus eighty-nine point two degrees Celsius, at Vostok Station in Antarctica, in July of 1983. In a sunless world, that stops being a record and starts looking like ordinary weather. Energy balance estimates suggest that within weeks to months, large parts of the continents would fall into that range and keep falling. I want to be careful here. The exact timing depends on assumptions about how fast the ocean gives up its heat and how quickly the dry, cloudless air lets it escape. Nobody has measured a sunless Earth. What I can say is that the direction is not in doubt, and neither is the order. First the air, then the land, then the sea. And the sea is where this story turns.

The ocean averages about three thousand seven hundred metres deep, and it is a gigantic store of heat. Its surface does freeze, and that matters, because ice is a poor conductor. A lid of ice slows the loss of heat from the water below, and the thicker the lid gets, the slower it grows. In a single year it thickens by metres, not kilometres. Beneath it, the deep ocean sits only a few degrees above freezing. The Earth's interior also leaks heat, about forty-seven terawatts of it. Spread over the whole surface, that is roughly nine hundredths of a watt per square metre, against the three hundred and forty we lost. It is nearly four thousand times weaker than the sun. For one year it is not what keeps the sea liquid. The heat already stored in the water does that. But over the thousands of years after, that trickle is what decides how long anything stays unfrozen. For now, the seas do not freeze solid.

And down in that dark water, life barely notices. Below about a thousand metres, there has never been any sunlight at all. In 1977, the submersible Alvin dived on the Galápagos Rift and found whole communities living around vents that pour out hot, chemical-rich water. There were clams, crabs, and tube worms taller than a person. None of them eat sunlight. Bacteria there make food from chemicals like hydrogen sulphide, and everything else eats the bacteria. These ecosystems have run without the sun for as long as there have been vents. Deeper still, a decade-long census by the Deep Carbon Observatory, published in 2018, estimated that life in the rock beneath us holds between fifteen and twenty-three billion tonnes of carbon. That is hundreds of times the carbon in all human beings. For a large share of Earth's microbes, a year without the sun is hardly an event.

But the air is about to do something strange.

Nitrogen makes up most of the atmosphere, and it turns to liquid at minus one hundred and ninety-six degrees Celsius. A planet with no sun and only its own interior heat would, left long enough, settle toward a temperature of a few tens of kelvin. A rough calculation using that geothermal figure gives about thirty-six kelvin. That is my own back-of-envelope estimate, not a published model, so treat it as a sense of scale. It says that in the very long run, the atmosphere would freeze out and lie on the ground as frost. But the long run is the key. Over a single year, the air gets bitterly cold, and it stays a gas. How much longer than a year it would take depends on assumptions about the ice and the ocean that I cannot settle for you, so I will not hand you a date.

Now fast-forward to the end of the year. The Earth has swung once around the sun, three hundred and sixty-five and a quarter days, and it sits exactly where it began. The tilt of twenty-three point four degrees would still give you seasons in theory, but there is no light to make them mean anything. The tides continue, because tides are gravity, and the sun's gravity still pulls on the oceans with a bit less than half the strength of the Moon's. Under the ice, the water still rises and falls twice a day.

For comparison, in 1816 the world suffered what history calls the Year Without a Summer, after the eruption of Mount Tambora the year before. Global temperatures dipped by only about half a degree Celsius. Crops still failed across Europe and North America, and food prices climbed. Our scenario does not remove a few percent of the sunlight. It removes all of it, for twelve months. There is no honest way to describe that as anything but the end of civilisation as we know it.

So here is the part you did not expect.

Everything so far has been about light. But the sun sends us something else, and it does not care about night or ice or ocean. Neutrinos. About sixty billion solar neutrinos pass through every square centimetre of you every second, and they have been doing it since before you were born. They are made in the core, and they ignore almost everything. They go through the Earth, through the ice, through kilometres of rock, as if the planet were not there. In our scenario, with the furnace still burning, they would keep coming through the whole year. You would be standing in the dark, freezing, with a signal from the sun passing through your chest that never stopped.

And this is what I find most remarkable. In the real, impossible version, where the core itself dies, those neutrinos are the only warning. They cross the distance in the same eight minutes and twenty seconds. Detectors on Earth, like Borexino in Italy and Super-Kamiokande in Japan, would see the signal vanish while the sun still looked perfectly bright, and would keep looking bright for thousands of years. The first people to learn that the sun was dead would be physicists reading a counter, with a blue sky still overhead.

That is the first surprise. The second is about the ending, and it is the one I most want you to argue with.

When the year is up, the light returns, and eight minutes and twenty seconds after that, the dark is over. You would expect a frozen world to stay frozen. Ice reflects sunlight, and a white planet bounces its warmth back into space. That is what happened in the snowball Earth episodes about seven hundred million years ago, when ice may have reached the equator, and it took a build-up of volcanic carbon dioxide over a very long time to break the freeze. But there is a catch in our scenario. What makes ice so reflective is snow. Fresh snow reflects around eighty to ninety percent of the light that hits it. And snow needs water vapour, which very cold air barely holds. A year of dry, bitter cold produces surprisingly little snow. Much of the ground would be bare, dark rock and soil, and the sea ice would be mostly bare too. So when the sun comes back, a large share of the light would be absorbed, not thrown away.

That argument suggests the thaw could take years, not millions of years. I want to be honest about it. It is a plausible argument, not a settled result. Climate modellers would argue about the details, especially how much frost and ice would form over the year, and nobody has run this experiment. If you think I have got it wrong, I would like to hear why.

So what comes back? The seeds in the soil, which can wait for years. The spores, and the microbes, and everything that never needed the sun in the first place. In a mountain on the Norwegian island of Svalbard, more than a million seed samples from around the world sit in a vault kept at about minus eighteen degrees Celsius. In our scenario it would get colder than that, but seeds stored dry are built to wait. The survivors near the geothermal fields and the reactors would come out into a world with a small, familiar disc in a pale sky, and they would have a great deal of work to do.

A year of darkness would end most of what we call civilisation, and nearly all of its large animals, and I do not want to soften that. But life, taken as a whole, would barely flinch. The deep ocean would keep its vents. The rocks would keep their microbes. And the sun, which we left burning underneath its blindfold, would simply be there again.

So I want to ask you. Suppose you got a warning, and you could pick one place on Earth to spend that dark year. Where would you go, and what is the first thing you would pack?
