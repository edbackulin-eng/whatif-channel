You are standing in an open field at noon. In your hand is a compass. The needle points north, as it has your whole life. Then it shivers. It swings left, then right, then drifts in a slow circle like a toy on a string. Nothing else happens. The sky is still blue. The sun is still warm. A bird on a fence post keeps singing.

That calm is the first surprise. The disaster in this story is not the one you picture, and it does not arrive where you are standing. It starts roughly sixty thousand kilometres away, and within three minutes it is at the top of your sky. Some of what follows is milder than the movies promised. Some of it is worse, and it is aimed at machines you rely on every hour of the day. So stay with me, because the most vulnerable place in this story is a few hundred kilometres over your head.

First, a plain statement. Earth cannot lose its magnetic field in an instant. The field is not a bar magnet buried in the planet. It is made by moving liquid metal. About twenty-nine hundred kilometres below your feet, a shell of molten iron and nickel, roughly twenty-two hundred kilometres thick, churns around a solid inner core. That inner core is about twelve hundred kilometres in radius, and its temperature is close to that of the surface of the Sun. Heat escaping from the core stirs the liquid, and the planet's spin twists the flow. Moving metal carries electric current, and current makes a magnetic field. This self-sustaining machine is called the geodynamo. Heat flows out of the core at somewhere around ten terawatts, though estimates differ by a factor of about three, and that disagreement is real. Switching off a machine that large, in a moment, would break known physics.

So here is the deal for this video. We cheat once. We pretend the dynamo stops and the field vanishes at a single instant. After that, we switch the physics back on and follow it honestly. At the end, I will show you why the cheat is not as far from reality as you think, and why one of the facts you will meet before then is stranger than the rest.

Before the clock starts, you need to know what the field is doing for you right now. At the surface it is weak. It measures between about twenty-five and sixty-five microtesla, which is roughly a hundredth of the strength of a fridge magnet. But it does not stop at the surface. It reaches far out into space, and it is shaped like a comet. Earth sits inside a bubble of magnetism, squeezed on the side facing the Sun and stretched into a long tail on the other side.

What presses on that bubble is the solar wind. The Sun constantly blows out charged particles, mostly protons and electrons. Their speed is usually between about two hundred and fifty and seven hundred and fifty kilometres per second, and a typical value is around four hundred. Charged particles cannot cross a magnetic field easily. They spiral around the field lines and get steered aside. On the sunward side, the boundary where the solar wind meets Earth's field sits at about ten Earth radii. Earth's radius is about sixty-three hundred and seventy kilometres, so that boundary is around sixty-four thousand kilometres out. That distance is the whole story of the first few minutes.

Now flip the switch.

In the first second, nothing you can sense changes. The magnetic field is invisible, odorless and silent. But your compass needle loses its direction. The same is true of every compass on every ship and aircraft on Earth.

Over the next few seconds, the protective bubble collapses, and the solar wind is no longer held at sixty-four thousand kilometres. The edge of the bubble is gone. Particles that were being steered around the planet now travel straight toward it. At four hundred kilometres per second, a particle covers sixty-four thousand kilometres in about a hundred and sixty seconds. That is under three minutes.

So here is the first number to remember. In less than three minutes, the solar wind reaches the top of the atmosphere. For comparison, the light from the Sun, which you can see, takes about eight minutes to arrive. The wind is slower than light, but the planet's defence against it is gone, so it arrives first in the form that matters.

And what happens when it arrives? This is where most people expect fire from the sky. Stay with that thought for a moment, because what actually happens is both smaller and stranger. The answer depends on a layer you have never thought of as a shield.

Your atmosphere is a shield. The air above every square metre of ground weighs about ten tonnes, which is equivalent to a column of water about ten metres deep. The solar wind particles are low in energy compared with cosmic rays, and they do not get through that much matter. They hit the thin upper air, hundreds of kilometres up, and stop. They ionise it. They heat it. They make it glow. But they do not reach you.

The more energetic particles are a different matter. Cosmic rays come from outside the solar system, and the occasional burst of solar energetic particles comes from the Sun. Some of them are fast enough to smash into atoms of the upper atmosphere and set off cascades of secondary particles. Some of those reach the ground. Today, the field deflects a share of the lower-energy ones, mostly near the equator. Without it, more would arrive.

How much more? On the ground, the effect would be a rise, not a catastrophe. The average person already receives about two point four millisieverts of radiation a year from natural sources. Cosmic rays account for roughly four-tenths of a millisievert of that. Take the field away, and that share goes up. Estimates vary with latitude and with how active the Sun is. But the thick air above you still does most of the work. That is a very different outcome from the one in the movies, where people are cooked in the open.

The place where it bites is high altitude. Aircrew already receive a measurable dose, and without the field, long flights near the poles and at cruising altitude would be a larger concern. Authorities would have to rethink how many hours crews can fly. That is a policy problem, not a survival problem. Keep the word altitude in mind, because the higher you go in this story, the worse it gets.

So the first hours pass. If you are on the ground, you probably notice nothing. But some living things notice at once.

Many animals use the field as a sense. Sea turtles, salmon, migrating songbirds, and even some insects appear to read it like a map. Exactly how they do it is not settled. One leading idea involves light-sensitive proteins called cryptochromes, in which the field subtly changes chemical reactions in the eye. Another involves tiny crystals of iron minerals. The evidence for each is real, and neither has been proven to be the whole answer. What is settled is the behaviour. Birds placed in artificial fields that point the wrong way head in the wrong direction. Some bacteria go further. Magnetotactic bacteria, first described in the nineteen seventies, build chains of magnetic crystals inside their bodies and use them to swim along the field toward the best patch of mud.

Now imagine the next migration season. In the first autumn without a field, birds that rely on it would not necessarily be lost. Many also use the stars, the Sun, the smell of the land and the shape of the coastline. But some species would struggle, and nobody can tell you exactly which. This is one of the honest unknowns in the story.

But that is the gentle part. The next part is not. Look up again. Not at the sky, but at what is in it.

By the end of the first day, the real casualty becomes clear. It is not on the ground. It is in orbit.

Earth's field does more than deflect the solar wind. It also traps particles. Around the planet are two great doughnuts of trapped radiation, the Van Allen belts, discovered in nineteen fifty-eight by Explorer One, the first American satellite. The inner belt reaches from about a thousand to six thousand kilometres above the surface. The outer belt reaches from about thirteen thousand to sixty thousand kilometres. They are made of protons and electrons held on looping paths by the field.

Without the field, nothing holds them. Over days, the belts drain away. That sounds like good news, and in one sense it is. The most hazardous radiation zones around the planet vanish. But the particles do not go away. They are not trapped any more, which means they are free to hit things, and some of them hit the top of the atmosphere, and some hit the spacecraft that fly through it.

And here is the larger point. The field was also keeping the far more numerous solar particles away from orbit. Without it, every satellite, from the lowest to the highest, sits in an open stream of radiation it was never designed for.

Consider what is up there. There are well over ten thousand active satellites. The navigation satellites that give your phone its position orbit at about twenty thousand two hundred kilometres, right where the outer belt's reach thins out. Communications satellites, weather satellites and spy satellites all rely on electronics that are hardened against a certain level of radiation. That level is set by the Van Allen belts and by the occasional storm, with the field in place.

What happens to a chip when a fast proton goes through it? It can flip a bit. It can latch up and draw too much current. Over time, it can degrade the material until the chip stops working. Satellite operators already see these events, and they plan for them. Without the field, the rate rises sharply, and the dose that builds up over a year rises with it. Some spacecraft would fail in weeks. Others would last months. Almost none would reach their planned lifetime.

There is a reminder of how real this is closer to home. The International Space Station orbits at about four hundred kilometres, inside the field's protection for most of its path. Even so, the crew there receive around eighty millisieverts over a six-month stay, which is roughly the dose you would collect from natural background on the ground over thirty years. Take away the field, and that number climbs. Astronauts would be recalled, or the station would have to be shielded in ways it was never built for.

So by the end of the first week, the world has a new problem. Your phone would still show a map for a while, because it can use cell towers. But the satellite signals that time the banking system, the power grid and the stock market would begin to fail. We would not notice the first failures. We would notice when the failures added up. And here is the question to hold on to. Is the sky itself changing as well?

It is. And the answer is beautiful, in a grim way.

The aurora would change. Today, the aurora is mostly a polar event, because the field funnels the solar wind toward the poles. Without the field to funnel it, particles would rain down everywhere, and the upper atmosphere would glow across the whole planet. For some days, nights at every latitude could be faintly lit. That is a spectacular sight with a bad reason behind it.

Now the grid. This part is genuinely uncertain, so I will give you both sides. Today, the worst space weather events damage power grids through the field. A solar storm shakes the magnetosphere, and the shaking induces currents in long conductors on the ground. In March of nineteen eighty-nine, one such storm collapsed the Hydro-Québec grid in about ninety seconds. Six million people lost power for around nine hours. In eighteen fifty-nine, the Carrington event set telegraph offices sparking and pushed the aurora down to the Caribbean. And in May of twenty twenty-four, the strongest geomagnetic storm in about twenty years lit up skies far south of their usual range.

So you might guess that a world without a field is a world with no such storms. That is partly true. The mechanism that sends currents into the ground depends on a magnetosphere to shake. But the solar wind would still meet the planet. It would still push against the upper atmosphere and make currents there. Whether those would induce damaging currents in the ground is not something anybody has measured, because it has never happened to a planet like ours. Researchers disagree about the answer. I would rather tell you that than pick the dramatic version.

There is a close cousin to Earth that shows us part of the answer. Venus has no strong magnetic field of its own. Yet the solar wind does not simply strip it bare. Venus makes a kind of substitute shield. Its upper atmosphere is ionised by sunlight, and that electrified layer drags the solar wind's own field around the planet, forming what is called an induced magnetosphere. On Venus, the boundary sits within a few hundred kilometres of the surface on the sunward side, as measured by the Pioneer Venus Orbiter. Earth, after the loss, would grow something like that. It would be a pale, thin copy of the real thing, hugging the planet very closely. That would protect the ground somewhat. It would not protect orbit at all.

Now let me move the clock forward. We are past the first week, and into the first years.

The years bring slower damage. Cosmic rays and solar particles striking the upper atmosphere break apart nitrogen and oxygen molecules. That chemistry makes nitrogen oxides, and nitrogen oxides destroy ozone. The ozone layer is what blocks the most damaging ultraviolet light from the Sun. Models of what a weak field would do to ozone disagree on size, and they depend a lot on how active the Sun is. The direction is not in dispute. Less ozone means more ultraviolet at the surface, more sunburn, more skin cancer, and more stress on crops and the plankton at the base of the ocean food chain. I will not give you a percentage, because the honest range is wide.

There is a second slow effect, and it matters to farmers. The change in cosmic rays also changes how many particles seed clouds, a link that is argued about and has never been shown to be strong. I mention it only so that you know the argument exists. Most climate scientists think the effect, if any, is small.

Meanwhile, human technology adapts. Pilots stop relying on magnetic headings and use inertial systems and GPS where it survives. Surveyors and geologists lose their reference. The maps of magnetic north that airport runways are named after become meaningless, and many runways would need renumbering over time. The little numbers painted at each end of a runway are based on the magnetic compass heading, so every airport on Earth gets a repainting job. It sounds trivial. It is a measure of how deeply this one invisible thing is woven into daily life.

Here is the next question, and it takes us from years to the long run. Does a planet without a magnetic field lose its air? That is the claim you have probably heard. Mars is the standard example.

Mars once had a global magnetic field. Rocks in its southern highlands are strongly magnetised, which tells us it did, and the field is thought to have died roughly four billion years ago. Today, Mars has an atmosphere with a surface pressure of only about six-tenths of one percent of Earth's. Most of that air is gone. The NASA mission called MAVEN has measured how it escapes. It found that the solar wind strips away something like a hundred grams of gas every second. That does not sound like much until you multiply it by billions of years.

So the standard story says that the field protected Earth's atmosphere and the lack of one doomed Mars. But the truth is more contested than that. Mars is small, with only about a third of Earth's surface gravity, so it loses gas more easily whatever its magnetism. It is also farther from the Sun, and it cooled faster. Venus is the better test, because Venus is about the same size as Earth and has no strong field of its own. Yet Venus has a crushing atmosphere, with a surface pressure about ninety-two times Earth's. It kept its air. What it lost was its water. Measurements from the Pioneer Venus mission show that the hydrogen left behind is enriched in the heavy form, deuterium, by more than a hundred times compared with Earth. That is the fingerprint of a world that once had far more water and lost the light hydrogen to space.

There is even a counter-argument that sounds backwards. A study published in Science Advances in twenty eighteen argued that an intrinsic field can funnel energy into the polar upper atmosphere and may in some conditions help ions escape, not stop them. The authors did not claim a magnetic field is harmful. They claimed the effect is not simple, and that the textbook story, field equals air, is too neat. Other researchers disagree with parts of that. The fair summary is this. A magnetic field changes how a planet loses atmosphere. It does not obviously decide whether the planet keeps one.

For Earth, the timescale also matters. Even in the worst reading, atmospheric loss over a human lifetime would be negligible. Over a million years, it would still not empty the sky. The question is about hundreds of millions of years and more, and the answer depends on factors we can only partly model.

This is a good moment to stop cheating, because the real Earth is already acting out part of this story.

The field is changing right now. The European Space Agency's Swarm satellites, launched in twenty thirteen, have mapped it in fine detail. Their data show that the global field has weakened by about nine percent over the past two hundred years. Over the South Atlantic, there is a wide region where the field is especially weak, called the South Atlantic Anomaly. Satellites passing through it suffer more radiation hits, and operators have to switch off sensitive instruments there. The anomaly is real, it is large, and it is evolving. Nobody knows whether it is the start of something or just a wobble in a field that has always wobbled.

And the field has gone through far worse. Rocks and sediments record its direction at the time they formed, and they show that the field flips. The north and south magnetic poles swap places. The last full flip, called the Brunhes–Matuyama reversal, happened about seven hundred and seventy thousand years ago. Reversals are irregular. Sometimes they come a few hundred thousand years apart, and sometimes much further apart. In the Cretaceous, the field stayed in one direction for nearly forty million years without flipping once.

The better question is what happens during a flip. Nobody has watched one with modern instruments, but the rock record says the field does not simply turn around. It becomes weak, tangled and multi-polar for some thousands of years, then re-forms in the opposite direction.

The best-studied recent event is the Laschamp excursion, about forty-one thousand years ago. During it, the field dropped to as low as about five percent of today's strength and, for roughly eight hundred years, pointed the wrong way. Humans were alive. Neanderthals were alive. A team led by Alan Cooper studied ancient kauri trees preserved in a New Zealand bog, some more than forty thousand years old, and used their growth rings to reconstruct how much cosmic radiation was reaching the atmosphere at the time. They proposed that the weakened field caused sharp shifts in climate and ozone, and that this may have played a part in extinctions of Ice Age animals in Australia and even in the decline of Neanderthals. That proposal is controversial, and many researchers think the evidence for the extinctions is weak, or that other causes explain them better. I include it because it is the closest thing we have to a real experiment, and the honest result is that the experiment is still being argued about.

Now, the turn I promised. Because the strangest part of this story is not about a field that vanishes. It is about what lived through a field that was almost gone.

About five hundred and sixty-five million years ago, in a period called the Ediacaran, a team led by John Tarduno's colleague Richard Bono measured the magnetism frozen into ancient igneous rocks. Their result, published in Nature Geoscience in twenty nineteen, is the weakest field ever recorded for Earth. It was about ten times weaker than the field you have today. Not a dip, not a wobble, but an extreme low that appears to have lasted a long time.

Here is what sits right beside that in the fossil record. The Ediacaran is when the first large, complex animals appear. Soft, strange creatures, some as long as a metre, lay on the sea floor. Within a few tens of millions of years, the Cambrian explosion arrived and nearly every major animal group on Earth today showed up in the fossils.

Now, correlation is not cause, so let me be careful. Bono and colleagues suggested that the field was weakest when the inner core had not yet formed. They also proposed that, as the inner core began to freeze and grow, the field strengthened again. When exactly the inner core began is itself disputed. Some studies put it around that time. Others place it far earlier, perhaps more than a billion years ago, and some of those argue that the Ediacaran low was a different kind of event. So that part is open.

A second suggestion goes further, and it is clearly speculative. A weak field would let hydrogen escape from the upper atmosphere faster, and that could leave more oxygen behind, which complex animals need. Some researchers find that attractive. Others point out that oxygen levels at the time are very hard to measure and that the link is far from proven.

What is not in doubt is the plain fact that the field was extremely weak when complex animals first appeared. Whatever the field does for life, it is not true that animals cannot exist without a strong one. And if you go back much further, Tarduno and colleagues found that the dynamo was already running at least three point four five billion years ago, very early in the history of life. Earth has had a field for most of its existence, and yet it has not always been strong.

So the surprise at the end is not that a dead field would be a disaster, or that it would be harmless. It is that the planet's long history gives us both answers. The ground-level world would probably go on, a little more sunburned, a little more confused. What would fail, quickly and expensively, is the layer of technology we built on the assumption that the field would always be there.

Which brings me to the question I would like to leave you with. If you could keep only one thing the field does for you, which would you choose? The compass in your pocket, the satellites over your head, the aurora over the poles, or the ozone layer that quietly protects your skin? Tell me in the comments, and tell me why. I read them all, and I would like to know which one you would give up last.
