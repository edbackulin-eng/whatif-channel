You are standing on the equator at noon. The sky is clear. A warm breeze moves across your face, and the ground under your feet feels as solid and still as it always has.

It is not still. Right now you are travelling east at about 1,670 kilometres per hour, carried around the planet's axis. You, the air, the oceans, the mountains, all of it moves together, so smoothly that you have never felt it once.

Now the planet stops turning. The rock stops. Nothing else does.

Before we go any further, a plain statement. This cannot happen. The spin of the Earth holds a vast amount of angular momentum, and angular momentum does not simply vanish. It can only be passed to something else, and nothing we know of could take all of it without wrecking the planet along the way. So this is a thought experiment. We let the spin disappear, we leave everything else exactly as it was, and then we switch the physics back on and watch what follows.

The first few seconds are brutal. The first few days are stranger. But the strangest part of the story does not arrive for years, and it involves the Moon doing something you would never expect. We will get there. First, let me show you how we even know the ground is moving, because you cannot feel it.

In 1851, the French physicist Léon Foucault hung a heavy ball from a very long wire inside the Panthéon in Paris and set it swinging. Slowly, the line of the swing began to turn. The pendulum was not turning. The floor was. At the latitude of Paris, the plane of the swing rotates by about 11.3 degrees every hour, so a full circle takes about 32 hours. The whole planet was turning beneath the pendulum, and Foucault had caught it in the act.

The Earth completes one turn relative to the distant stars in 23 hours, 56 minutes and 4 seconds. That is about four minutes shorter than the 24 hours on your clock, because the Earth is also travelling around the Sun, and it has to turn a little extra each day to bring the Sun back to the same spot in the sky.

How fast you are carried depends on where you stand. The equator is 40,075 kilometres around, and it goes round once in that time, which is where the 1,670 kilometres per hour comes from. Move north or south and the circle you travel gets smaller. In London you are moving at about 1,040 kilometres per hour. At the poles you are doing nothing more than turning slowly on the spot. Keep that in mind, because it decides who is hit hardest when the ground stops. And hold on to one other number, the speed of sound, because the wind is about to cross it.

So, second one. The rock under you has stopped. Everything on top of it has not. That includes you, every building, every tree, the oceans and the entire atmosphere. This is plain inertia. Anything moving keeps moving until a force acts on it, and at the equator the thing that was carrying all of it has just let go. If you are inside a sturdy building, the building is fixed to the stopped ground and you are not. You meet the wall at the speed you were already travelling, which is far beyond anything a human body survives. Outside, you are no better off, because you are about to meet the air.

The air is the real story. Relative to the ground, the wind at the equator is now about 465 metres per second. The speed of sound in air at 15 degrees Celsius is about 340 metres per second. So this wind is blowing faster than sound, at roughly 1.4 times the speed of sound. For comparison, the strongest surface wind gust ever measured by a weather station was 408 kilometres per hour, on Barrow Island in Australia, during Tropical Cyclone Olivia in 1996. The wind you are standing in is about four times faster than that.

How hard does that push? The force of moving air grows with the square of its speed. The simple formula, half the air density times the speed squared, gives about 130 kilopascals at sea level. A calm day has an ordinary air pressure of 101 kilopascals. At these speeds air compresses and the simple formula stops being reliable, so treat that as a guide to the scale and nothing more. But the scale is clear. No tree, house or bridge is built for this. Topsoil is stripped from the ground. Anything standing at the equator is flattened and carried away.

This is not a thin layer, either. Half the mass of the atmosphere lies below about 5.5 kilometres, so the wind is a deep slab of air, scouring the planet from the surface up. Away from the equator it weakens. At 45 degrees latitude it is about 1,180 kilometres per hour, just under the speed of sound, which is about 1,225 kilometres per hour. At the poles there is no wind at all. So the planet splits into a killing belt around its middle and calmer places at its ends. But the calm will not last, because the sea is coming, and it is moving about twice as fast as a tsunami crosses the deep ocean.

Minutes in. The oceans cover about 71 percent of the surface and are about 3,700 metres deep on average. Water has inertia too. All of it was moving east with the ground, and now the ground has stopped. A tsunami crosses the deep ocean at around 800 kilometres per hour. This is not a wave. It is the whole body of the sea, moving at up to 1,670 kilometres per hour.

It keeps going until something stops it. Eastward-moving water runs into the eastern edges of the ocean basins, which are the coasts that face west, like the Pacific coasts of the Americas and the Atlantic coasts of Europe and Africa. The water piles up, climbs the land, and rebounds, and the basins slosh back and forth for a long time. How far inland it goes depends on coastlines and terrain in ways I will not pretend to calculate. What is certain is that low coastal land is scoured, and that high ground is the only safe ground.

To give you a sense of scale, the rotational energy stored in the Earth is about 2 times 10 to the 29 joules. All of humanity uses about 6 times 10 to the 20 joules in a year. So the spin holds more than 300 million years of everything we burn, run and switch on. In our scenario, the rock's share of that energy simply goes missing, which is one more way of saying the premise is impossible. What you feel is the energy left in the air and the sea.

That wind and water are the fast part. The slow part hangs above them, and it is about to do something it has never done in the history of our species. Over hours and days, friction with the ground drags the lowest air to a halt, though the upper atmosphere keeps moving for longer, and nobody can say precisely how long. Meanwhile, look up.

Normally the sun crosses the sky at 15 degrees per hour. That is gone. The only thing moving the sun across your sky now is the Earth's trip around it, which shifts the Sun by just under one degree per day. The Sun is about half a degree wide, so it takes about 13 hours to move its own width. At the equator, it stays above the horizon for about six months, then spends about six months below it. The day is now as long as the year.

For a sense of how strange that is, look at other worlds. Venus takes about 117 Earth days from one sunrise to the next. Mercury takes 176. The Moon takes 29.5. Our planet would now take 365. And the Moon shows what a long day does. At the lunar equator, the surface reaches about 127 degrees Celsius in the afternoon and falls to about minus 173 at night. Earth has air and oceans to move heat around, so it would not be that bad. But no Earth in history has had a day this long, and how much those oceans and winds can soften it is something scientists argue about. I will show you both sides soon. First, though, the weather goes, and it goes for a reason you have probably never thought about.

Weather depends on the spin more than you might think. Moving air is deflected, to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the south, because the ground turns underneath it. This is the Coriolis effect, and it is what twists storms into spirals. It is so important that hurricanes almost never form within about 5 degrees of the equator, where the effect is too weak. Without spin, it is gone everywhere, so no hurricanes, no cyclones and no spiral at all. That sounds like good news. It is not.

The same deflection also organises the air into belts, the trade winds, the westerlies and the jet streams. A classic 1980 calculation by Isaac Held and Arthur Hou showed that on a slowly rotating planet, the tropical circulation stretches all the way to the poles. Picture one enormous sea breeze. Air rises over the hot side, travels high above the ground toward the cold side, sinks, and flows back along the surface. Not a pleasant day to be outside, but a simple, steady system.

The oceans lose their organisation too. The Gulf Stream carries about 30 million cubic metres of water every second through the Florida Straits, more than twenty times the flow of every river on Earth combined. The oceanographer Henry Stommel showed in 1948 that strong western currents like this one exist because the Earth rotates. Take the spin away, and the reason for such a current goes with it.

But none of that matches what happens next, which almost nobody sees coming. The oceans themselves leave the equator.

The Earth is not a sphere. It bulges at the equator because it spins. The equatorial radius is 6,378 kilometres and the polar radius is 6,357 kilometres, a difference of about 21 kilometres. The oceans are part of that shape. The spin has pulled water toward the equator, and sea level follows the bulge. When the spin stops, the solid rock keeps its bulge for now, because rock is stiff. Water is not. Water runs downhill, and what counts as downhill has just changed. The new low ground is at the poles.

Simple models put the shift in sea level at several kilometres. That is enough to drain much of the equatorial ocean, leaving a wide belt of dry seabed, and to drown the high latitudes under water far deeper than any flood in human history. The world turns into a ring of land around its middle, with deep oceans capping both ends. How long this takes is contested. The rock does flow, but slowly. Scandinavia is still rising today from the weight of ice that melted roughly ten thousand years ago. So the solid Earth might take tens of thousands of years or longer to give up its bulge, and the seas may sit in the wrong place for that whole time.

A ring of new land and a sun that barely moves. It sounds almost peaceful. But there is a danger on a slower clock than wind and water, and it begins nearly three thousand kilometres below your feet. First, let me show you something odd about the sky.

Weeks pass. Look at the sun again. Today it rises in the east and sets in the west, because the ground turns eastward beneath it. Now the only thing moving the sun is Earth's orbit, which carries the Sun eastward against the stars. So the Sun creeps slowly from west to east. It rises in the west and sets in the east, once a year. Venus already does something like this, though it gets there by spinning backward.

And the line between day and night, which astronomers call the terminator, no longer races across the ground. At the equator it moves about 110 kilometres per day, which is about 4.6 kilometres per hour. That is slightly slower than a brisk walk. Hold that thought, because the best news in this story is hiding in that number.

Now the slow danger. Nearly three thousand kilometres under your feet, the rocky mantle ends and a layer of liquid metal begins. This outer core is mostly iron, it is about 2,260 kilometres thick, and its churning generates the magnetic field. At the surface that field measures between about 25 and 65 microtesla, and it deflects the solar wind, the stream of charged particles that the Sun throws off.

The geodynamo needs two things: heat to drive the convection, and rotation to organise it. The Coriolis effect twists the rising and sinking metal into spiralling columns, and those are good at making a coherent field. So what happens if the planet stops? Here the answer is not settled. A fluid core has inertia of its own, so it would keep turning while the rock around it stopped, and that difference in motion could keep a dynamo going for some time, maybe even drive it harder. But once the core also slows, the organising effect is gone, and the field could fade, or become something quite different. Nobody has a confident answer, because nobody has ever watched a planetary dynamo without rotation. So what does a fading field do to a planet with an atmosphere? We have one warning from next door.

Mars lost its global magnetic field billions of years ago. Today its atmosphere has a surface pressure about 160 times lower than ours. NASA's MAVEN spacecraft has measured the solar wind still stripping gas from the Martian upper atmosphere. But that took billions of years. Earth's atmosphere is far thicker, and it would not disappear in a human lifetime. What would change sooner is the radiation reaching high altitudes, the reach of the aurora, and the safety of satellites and power grids. A weaker field is a slow problem for the long run and a fast one for our technology.

That covers the sky. Now think about your wristwatch, because the trouble there is odder than you would guess. Your phone keeps working, because the second is not defined by the spin. It is defined as 9,192,631,770 cycles of the radiation from a caesium atom. But the day comes loose. Since 1972, timekeepers have added 27 leap seconds to keep clocks matched to the planet's rotation, and in 2022 the world's measurement authorities agreed to stop doing that by 2035. In our scenario, the gap is no longer a fraction of a second. It is the whole sky.

Your body is tuned to a day, too. Kept in dim light without clocks, people's inner rhythm averages about 24 hours and 11 minutes, according to a well-known 1999 study. Light is what resets it every morning. Six months of unbroken sun would break that reset. People do live through long polar days, since at the North Pole the sun stays up about six months each year, but that is one place, not a world.

So far this is a story of loss. A scorched side, a frozen side, a fading shield. It is the version you will find in most videos on this subject. It is also the naive one, and the research on slowly turning planets says something different.

Start with the night. A common fear is that the dark side gets so cold that the air freezes out and falls as snow. In 1997, Manoj Joshi, Robert Haberle and Ray Reynolds simulated planets with a permanent night side and found that an atmosphere with a surface pressure of only about 0.1 bar carries enough heat across to prevent that collapse. Earth's air is about ten times thicker than that. The night side would get very cold, but the air would not rain out.

Now the day side. In 2013, Jun Yang, Nicolas Cowan and Dorian Abbot ran climate models of slowly rotating planets and found a self-correcting mechanism. Where the Sun stands overhead, warm moist air rises and builds a thick, bright cloud deck, and the hotter it gets, the more cloud forms. The clouds reflect sunlight back to space and shield the surface. In their results, such a planet can stay habitable at levels of sunlight that would make a fast spinner too hot. The pattern that results is sometimes called an eyeball world, a round patch of open ocean under the clouds, ringed by ice.

I owe you the caveat. Those models are mostly of planets locked with one face to their star, or turning in days or weeks. Ours would turn once a year, and the cloud shield would have to crawl across the surface with the Sun. So this is an extrapolation, and I would be misleading you to call it a prediction. But it is enough to say that the scorched and frozen picture is not the only reasonable one.

Which brings me back to that walking pace. Around the line between day and night, the sun sits low, the air stays mild, and the dawn creeps forward at about 4.6 kilometres per hour. Researchers studying planets with a permanent day side and night side have long suggested that the twilight band between them is a promising place for life. On a stopped Earth, with a ring of new land around the equator, a person could walk along with the dawn, and spend a year in the morning.

Now the turn I promised. All of this assumes the Earth stays stopped. It would not. To see why, you have to know why it spins at the rate it does, because that rate is not a constant of nature. It is a loan, and the Moon has been collecting on it for billions of years.

Every day, the Moon raises tides, and those tides rub against the seabed and the shorelines. That friction takes a little spin from the Earth and adds it to the Moon's orbit. The result is that each day gets longer by about 1.8 milliseconds per century, and the Moon drifts away from us at about 3.8 centimetres per year, a rate measured by bouncing lasers off mirrors left by the Apollo astronauts.

Run it backwards. Fossil corals from about 380 million years ago record roughly 400 days in a year. Layered rocks in China show that 1.4 billion years ago the day was about 18.7 hours long. Right after the giant impact that formed the Moon, the day may have lasted somewhere between two and five hours, depending on which model you trust. The Earth began life spinning fast, and has been slowing ever since.

Now play the film from the stopped state. A planet at rest, with a Moon overhead, does not stay at rest. The Moon raises a tidal bulge, and the bulge lags a little behind the Moon, because rock and water take time to respond. With the Earth stopped, the Moon sweeps across your sky once every 27.3 days, and the high tides come about 13.7 days apart. The Moon's pull on the lagging bulge twists the Earth gently forward, in the same direction the Moon orbits. The stopped Earth starts to turn again, without anyone's help. The Sun's tides push the same way, on a yearly rhythm. And in return, the Moon, which has been gaining energy from us for billions of years, starts to pay some back, and moves a little closer.

How fast would this happen? I cannot give you a confident timescale. The torque depends on how much energy the oceans and the rock dissipate, and in this world the oceans are in the wrong place. But it is slow, far slower than any span of human history. The stopped planet is not a permanent state. It is a pause. The spin you took for granted is a leftover from the violence of the Earth's birth, being spent a few milliseconds a century, and in our scenario the account is wiped clean and the Moon begins to lend it back.

So here is the whole story. In the first second, the air becomes a supersonic wind. In minutes, the sea runs east. In days, the weather machine unravels and the oceans drift toward the poles. In weeks, the sun starts to rise in the west. In years, the magnetic field is in doubt, a cloud shield may or may not save the day side, and a slow clock of tides starts winding the planet up again.

Here is what I want to know. Suppose the cloud models are right and the twilight band is livable, but the magnetic field fades anyway. Would you take the walk, keeping pace with the dawn at 4.6 kilometres per hour, or would you stay put and take your chances at noon?
