You are standing on a beach at dusk, and the sky is wrong. The Moon is rising over the water, bright and familiar, the way it has risen for every human who ever lived. But behind it, higher and farther out, a second disc is climbing. It is smaller. It is paler. It is unmistakably another world, hanging over the ocean as if it had always been there.

The tide pulling at your ankles feels a little stronger than you remember. The shadows on the sand have two edges, one sharp and one faint. And somewhere under your feet, the planet has just started doing something it has never done before.

Let us be honest about the premise first. Earth cannot simply acquire a second Moon like ours. A body that size does not arrive and settle into orbit. To be captured, it has to lose energy, and a passing world the size of our Moon would almost certainly either sail on by or hit us. So we cheat. We say the second Moon is simply there, the same size and the same mass as the first. Then we switch the physics back on and watch what happens. That cheat will matter at the end, because the physics has a way of answering it.

Here are the rules. Our Moon stays where it is, about 384,400 kilometres away, and it is about 3,500 kilometres across. The newcomer is a twin in size and mass, but it orbits farther out, at about 700,000 kilometres. That is nearly twice as far. Hold that number in your head, because the whole story turns on whether a number like it can survive.

We start with the first second. Nothing visible happens. Gravity does not wait for you to notice it. The moment the second Moon exists, it tugs on every ocean, every mountain and every cubic metre of rock. The Moon has about one eighty-first of the mass of Earth, so the pull on the planet as a whole is tiny. But it is not nothing, and it is not constant. We will come back to that.

Light is slower than gravity's reach. The light from our familiar Moon takes about 1.3 seconds to reach you. The light from the new one takes about 2.3 seconds. So in the first few seconds you are already seeing a sky that is slightly out of date, and the second Moon is the more out of date of the two. Its light left before you had finished asking what it was.

Now the brightness. A full Moon feels bright, but it is a poor mirror. It is roughly 400,000 times dimmer than the Sun. The second Moon, being twice as far, covers a smaller patch of sky. Its apparent area is about a quarter of the first, so it delivers about a quarter of the light. Your night does not become day. It becomes a night with a brighter lamp and a dimmer lamp, and when both are full, a noticeably better one.

Within the first few minutes you start to notice how it looks. Our Moon spans about half a degree of sky, roughly the width of your little finger held at arm's length. The second Moon is only about 0.28 degrees across, so it looks about half as wide. It is a smaller coin, thrown from a longer distance. And it moves differently. Our Moon drifts against the stars by about 13 degrees a day. The second, on its much longer orbit, drifts by only about 5 degrees. Watch both for a few minutes and you can see them slowly sliding past one another. Stay with that, because the sliding is where the trouble starts.

There is one more thing the sky offers in these first minutes, and it is the one astronomers would care about most. Our Moon and the Sun both span about half a degree. That is a coincidence. The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon, and about 400 times farther away. It is why a total solar eclipse is possible at all. The second Moon, at 0.28 degrees, is too small. It can cross the face of the Sun, but it can never cover it. Every eclipse it makes is a ring of fire, never a total darkness. So this world gets more eclipses, and fewer of the ones people travel across oceans to see.

That brings us to the first hours, and the ocean. Tides are not caused by the Moon pulling the water towards it. They are caused by a difference. The side of Earth facing the Moon is pulled harder than the centre, and the centre is pulled harder than the far side. That stretches the oceans into two bulges, one facing the Moon and one pointing away. As Earth turns beneath those bulges, you get two high tides a day. Because the Moon moves along its orbit as well, the rhythm is not 12 hours but 12 hours and 25 minutes.

The strength of that stretching falls off with the cube of distance. That is a harsh rule. Double the distance and the effect drops by a factor of eight. So the second Moon, at about 1.8 times the distance, raises a tide only about 17 percent as strong as our Moon does. The Sun, which is enormously heavier but vastly farther, raises a tide a little under half as strong as the Moon's.

Add them up and you can see the new ocean. On a normal spring tide, when the Sun and Moon line up, the Sun adds roughly 46 percent to the lunar tide. With the second Moon lined up too, you add another 17 percent. The biggest tides would be about 11 or 12 percent higher than today's biggest tides. That does not sound like much, until you remember where the biggest tides already are.

In the open ocean, the tide is less than a metre. But in a funnel-shaped bay, the water piles up. At the Bay of Fundy in Canada, the tidal range is more than 15 metres. Add a bit over 11 percent and you get nearly two extra metres of water in the highest places, twice a day. Harbours, tidal flats, salt marshes and the animals that live on them would all feel it. And it would not be steady. Because the two Moons slide past one another, the tides would swell and shrink on a new, longer rhythm. I will show you that rhythm in a moment, but first, the days.

Over the first few days, life responds. Many animals read the Moon like a calendar. On the Great Barrier Reef, corals release their eggs and sperm in a mass spawning that happens on a few nights after a full Moon in late spring. Predators and prey on land adjust their hunting to how much moonlight there is. With two Moons, the darkest nights get a little less dark, and the brightest get a little brighter. The signal that animals use to keep time becomes muddier. A coral that waits for a clear cue now sees two. Some would adapt. Some, in the short term, would simply miss the party.

Now the weeks. Our Moon takes 27.3 days to go around Earth relative to the stars. The phases take a little longer, 29.5 days, because Earth is also moving around the Sun. By Kepler's third law, a world at 700,000 kilometres takes about 67 days to complete a circuit. So the second Moon runs through its phases on its own slow clock. And the two Moons overtake one another roughly every six or seven weeks. Every time they do, they line up, and their tides add up. They also rise at different times. Our Moon rises about 50 minutes later each day. The second rises only about 22 minutes later. Your night sky becomes a slow dance with no repeat for a long time.

That dance is lovely. It is also the first sign of a problem, because every time one world passes the other, they tug on each other. And those tugs do not average out for free.

So let us move to years. The first thing that changes is the length of the day. Tides do not only move water. They also drag. The tidal bulge in the ocean does not sit exactly under the Moon. Earth spins faster than the Moon orbits, so the bulge is carried slightly ahead. That misaligned bulge pulls the Moon forward, and the Moon pulls back on it. The result is that Earth's rotation slows and the Moon gets flung to a higher orbit.

This is real and measured. Mirrors left on the Moon by Apollo astronauts and Soviet missions let scientists bounce lasers off it and time the return. The Moon is moving away from us at about 3.8 centimetres a year, about as fast as your fingernails grow. Earth's day is lengthening by about 1.7 milliseconds per century.

That sounds small, but it adds up. Fossil corals from about 400 million years ago, in the Devonian period, show daily growth ridges. Counting them gives roughly 400 days in a year. The year was the same length. The days were shorter, about 22 hours. The Moon was closer, and Earth spun faster.

Now, what does a second Moon do to that clock? Here the cube law is replaced by something harsher. The braking torque from tides falls off with the sixth power of distance. At 1.8 times the distance, the second Moon adds only about 3 percent to the braking. It is the wrong place to look for dramatic change. The day would lengthen only slightly faster than it does now. If you were hoping for a planet with a sluggish, drowsy rotation, a distant second Moon does not deliver.

The next effect is more subtle, and scientists still argue about it. Earth is tilted by about 23.4 degrees, and the tilt wobbles gently between about 22 and 24.5 degrees over a cycle of about 41,000 years. That wobble is part of why ice ages come and go. In 1993, the French astronomer Jacques Laskar published calculations suggesting that without our Moon, Earth's tilt would wander chaotically, anywhere from nearly zero to about 85 degrees. A planet like that would have wild, unpredictable seasons.

That finding became famous. It is also not settled. Later work, including a 2012 study by Jack Lissauer and colleagues, found that without the Moon, Earth's tilt would vary much more mildly than Laskar's first estimate. So does a Moon stabilise the tilt? Probably, to some extent. By how much is still debated. A second Moon would add its own steadying pull, but nobody can say it is needed.

That brings us back to the number I told you to remember. A second Moon at 700,000 kilometres. Earth has a region where its gravity beats the Sun's, called the Hill sphere, and it reaches out to about 1.5 million kilometres. But you cannot fill all of it with stable orbits. For orbits that go the same way as Earth spins, studies of the three-body problem find that stability begins to fail at about half of the Hill radius, roughly 730,000 kilometres. Our second Moon sits at 700,000. It is just inside the edge. That is already uncomfortable.

Then there is the other problem, the one the sky was already hinting at. Two worlds orbiting the same planet cannot be too close together. A common rule of thumb, worked out by the astronomer Brett Gladman in 1993, says two bodies in orbit around a heavier one stay stable only if they are separated by at least about three and a half of their mutual Hill radii. It is a rule of thumb, not a proof. But apply it to two Moon-mass worlds around Earth, and the outer one must be at about 800,000 kilometres or more. That is beyond the point where single orbits begin to fail. In other words, there may be no stable place at all for an identical twin. Our second Moon at 700,000 sits inside the danger zone.

What happens then? We cannot say exactly, and anyone who gives you a date is guessing. Chaotic systems do not announce a timetable. The orbits could hold for a very long time, perhaps millions of years, and then go wrong. When they do, the likely endings are dramatic. The two Moons could collide with each other. One could be flung out of the Earth system entirely. Or one could be kicked onto an orbit that grazes Earth. That last possibility is the one that ends the story for everything alive.

Is there a safer place to put a second Moon? There is a famous one. Between Earth and the Moon there are two special points, called L4 and L5, sitting 60 degrees ahead of and behind the Moon on its orbit. A small body can stay there almost indefinitely, provided the heavier of the two main bodies is at least about 25 times the mass of the lighter. Earth is about 81 times the Moon. So Earth and the Moon pass that test with room to spare.

And there may be real objects there. In 1961, the Polish astronomer Kazimierz Kordylewski reported faint patches of light at those points, thought to be clouds of dust. In 2018, a Hungarian team reported polarised light consistent with dust clouds there. Other astronomers want more confirmation, and the clouds are still debated. But the standard maths assumes the trailing body is a speck. A Moon-sized world is not a speck, and nobody can promise that it would stay.

So the physics gives a clear answer to our cheat. A second Moon like ours does not fit well. And that is where the surprise comes in, because it turns out the question is not hypothetical. Earth may already have had a second Moon, and you have seen what is left of it.

Look at the Moon with a telescope. The near side is a landscape of dark plains. The far side, which we never see from Earth, is almost entirely bright, rugged highlands, and its crust is noticeably thicker. Nobody has fully explained why the two faces are so different.

In 2011, Martin Jutzi and Erik Asphaug published a proposal in the journal Nature. After the giant impact that made the Moon, about 4.5 billion years ago, debris in orbit may have formed not one body but two. A smaller companion would have shared the Moon's orbit, sitting at one of those stable points we just talked about. Over time, the gravity of Earth and the Moon would have nudged it out. Eventually the two collided.

The key detail is the speed. The simulations suggest the impact was gentle, about two and a half kilometres per second. That is slow for a space collision. The companion did not blast a crater. It splashed onto the surface, spread out, and froze, plastering a thick layer of new crust over one side of the Moon. That layer, they argue, is the far side highlands.

This is a hypothesis, not a fact. Other explanations exist, and the debate is open. But think about what it means. The same physics that makes a second Moon unstable in our scenario may have already played out. A second Moon formed. It did not last. And its remains may be sitting on the sky you look at every night, hidden by the fact that the Moon always shows us the same face.

So go back to the beach. The sky has two Moons, and for a while it is the most beautiful thing you have ever seen. The tides are a little higher and the nights are a little brighter. The eclipses are all rings, never darkness. The days stretch at almost the same rate as before. And the whole time, the two Moons are tugging at each other, slowly, silently, building towards an answer that nobody can compute exactly. The most likely outcome of a sky like this is not a permanent wonder. It is a countdown.

And the real sky tells the same story with one Moon, and a scar on its far side that may be the grave of the other.

So here is my question for you. If you could choose, would you rather live under a sky with two Moons that cannot last, or keep the one steady Moon we have, knowing it is slowly leaving us? Tell me in the comments, and tell me where you would put the second one if you had to.
