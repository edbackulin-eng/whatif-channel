You are standing in a field at night. The air is cool and still. The stars are exactly where they have always been, and nothing looks wrong. But somewhere out there, about one point four times the distance from you to the Sun, there is an object with roughly ten times the mass of the Sun. It is moving at thirty kilometres every second. It is only about sixty kilometres across, so it covers its own width every two seconds. And you cannot see it at all.

This is a black hole, and it is passing through the solar system. In this video we follow what happens to the Sun, to the planets, and to you, from the first second to the last year. And near the end, the story stops being about this black hole at all. Stay for that part, because it changes what the word danger means here.

First, one honest note about the premise. This is not impossible. No law of physics forbids a black hole from wandering into our neighbourhood. But it is absurdly unlikely. The nearest black hole we have found, a system called Gaia BH1, is about fifteen hundred and sixty light years away, and it weighs about nine point six Suns. A second one, Gaia BH3, announced in 2024, is about nineteen hundred light years away and weighs about thirty three Suns. Astronomers think the Milky Way holds something like a hundred million black holes made from dead stars, though that estimate is uncertain by a wide margin. Almost all of them are invisible, because a lone black hole gives off no light of its own.

Even ordinary stars almost never come close. The closest known flyby is a faint star called Scholz's star, which passed roughly seventy thousand years ago, at a distance of about eight tenths of a light year. That is around fifty thousand times the distance from Earth to the Sun. So we are going to cheat on the odds and keep the physics honest.

Here are the rules of our scenario. The black hole has ten solar masses, close to Gaia BH1. It arrives at thirty kilometres per second, which is ordinary for the Sun's part of the galaxy, where neighbouring stars drift past one another at tens of kilometres per second. At its closest, it is one astronomical unit from the Sun. That is about a hundred and fifty million kilometres, the same as the radius of Earth's orbit. At that moment, Earth is a quarter of the way round its orbit from the black hole, which is why it sits about one point four astronomical units from you. I chose this path. Other paths give other endings, and I will tell you where that matters.

Now, the most common mistake. A black hole is not a vacuum cleaner. Far from it, its gravity is exactly the gravity of any object with the same mass. If you swapped the Sun for a black hole of one solar mass, Earth would keep orbiting just as before, in the dark and the cold. The hole itself is tiny. A black hole has a radius of about two point nine five kilometres for every solar mass, so ours is about thirty kilometres in radius. Nothing falls in unless it passes within that distance. The real danger is not the hole. It is the mass. Ten Suns of gravity are arriving in a place built for one.

Let us start the clock.

Second zero. Nothing happens, and that is the first surprise. The black hole is pulling on Earth about five times harder than the Sun does. But it pulls on you, on the ground, on the oceans and on the planet's core by almost exactly the same amount, so everything falls together. This is free fall. You feel nothing, just as astronauts on the space station feel nothing, even though gravity up there is still about ninety percent of what it is on the ground.

What you can feel is only the difference in pull from one side of Earth to the other. That difference is called a tide. At this distance, the black hole's tide on Earth is only somewhat stronger than the Moon's. The oceans rise and fall a little more than usual. That is all.

Overhead, one oddity. Light from a distant star that passes close behind the hole is bent around it. This is gravitational lensing, which Einstein predicted and which was first measured during the solar eclipse of 1919. If a star lined up perfectly, you would see a thin ring of light a few arcminutes across, about a seventh the width of the full Moon. But on almost every night, nothing lines up, so the black hole stays hidden. In the first few seconds you have learned the most unsettling fact of this video. The most dangerous object in the sky does not look like anything.

But the real trouble is not at your feet. It is in the Sun. To see why, we have to leave the field and look at the whole system.

So, minutes. Sunlight takes about eight minutes and twenty seconds to reach Earth, so every ray that touches your skin left the Sun before you had even noticed the problem. And the Sun is fine. It is exactly as bright as it was. The Sun is about six hundred and ninety six thousand kilometres in radius, and the black hole is more than two hundred times farther away than that. Nothing in the Sun's core knows it is there.

But gravity does not need to touch something to move it. At this moment the black hole pulls the Sun ten times harder than the Sun pulls Earth. That is simple arithmetic, because the black hole has ten times the mass and sits at the same distance. And the Sun and Earth do not fall toward the black hole at the same rate. The Sun, being closer, is pulled harder. So, measured from the Sun, Earth feels a new force, about eight times as strong as the Sun's usual pull on it, and pointing in a different direction.

Eight times. Earth's orbit is not about to wobble. It is about to be rewritten. How fast? That is the next question, and the answer is why this scenario is so different from the movies.

Notice what this means for the far end of the system. Neptune takes about one hundred and sixty five years to go round the Sun, so over a few weeks it barely moves at all. But the Sun beneath it is being dragged sideways. From Neptune's point of view, the centre of its orbit has suddenly lurched. That is the quiet way these encounters break a planetary system. The outer worlds are not hit. They are simply left behind.

Hours. The black hole is now moving about one hundred and eight thousand kilometres every hour. If that extra push on Earth stayed about the same, then after one hour Earth would be about three hundred kilometres off its old path. After six hours, roughly eleven thousand kilometres, close to the width of the planet. And you still feel nothing. There is no jolt and no rumble. The ground is steady, because the whole planet, you included, is falling along a new route together.

The only people who notice are the ones who track spacecraft and planets. Positions across the solar system are known so precisely that an error like this would show up in the data within hours. By the end of the first day, every planetary scientist alive would be looking at the same plots, and having the same thought. The solar system is no longer behaving like itself.

Days. In one day the black hole moves about two point six million kilometres, nearly seven times the distance to the Moon. In that same day, the extra push would change Earth's velocity by about four kilometres per second. Earth circles the Sun at about thirty kilometres per second. And the speed it needs to escape the Sun's grip, at its distance, is about forty two. Keep that push going for a week, and the changes add up to nearly the whole of Earth's orbital speed.

I need to be careful here. The black hole keeps moving, so the push keeps changing direction. The real answer needs a computer to follow every planet at once, and the result depends strongly on the exact path. Simulations of stellar flybys, going back decades, show a broad pattern. Planets farther out than the closest approach are the most exposed, and a flyby that comes within a few times a planet's orbital distance tends to throw it onto a stretched orbit or out of the system entirely. So for Earth, there are really three endings.

In the first, Earth stays bound to the Sun but on a long, stretched orbit, with seasons so extreme that you would swing between scorching and freezing over a few years. In the second, Earth is thrown out of the solar system altogether. In the third, a very rare one, Earth passes so close to the hole that it is captured, or shredded. The chance of that is tiny, because Earth is only about twelve thousand seven hundred kilometres wide, and the black hole has hundreds of millions of kilometres in which to miss it. For the rest of this video, I will follow the second ending, because it is the most instructive. Just keep in mind that it is a choice, not a prediction.

What about the Moon? It most likely stays with Earth. The Moon sits in a tight orbit, about three hundred and eighty four thousand kilometres out, and Earth's grip on it is far stronger than anything the black hole can do at this distance. So Earth and Moon leave the Sun together, like a pair of skaters holding hands.

And the Sun? Here the arithmetic is surprising. At this speed and this closest distance, the Sun and the black hole swing around each other like two dancers who barely touch. The path of the Sun, as seen from the black hole, bends through about a hundred and sixty five degrees. That is almost a U turn. The Sun does not fall in. It gets flung onto a different road through the galaxy, carrying whichever planets it managed to keep.

Which planets are those? Think about time. The black hole's close approach lasts about two months, because that is how long it takes to cross a distance equal to its closest approach to the Sun. Mercury goes round the Sun in eighty eight days. So Mercury has the best odds, which is not the same as good odds. But Jupiter takes nearly twelve years to complete an orbit. To Jupiter, the whole encounter is a blur, a sudden shove, and the Sun is yanked away from it. The giants are not safe because they are big. They are exposed because they are slow.

First, a quick look at the Sun's road. The Sun circles the Milky Way once every two hundred and thirty million years or so, at about two hundred and twenty kilometres per second. A kick of a few tens of kilometres per second changes the shape of that orbit, but it is nowhere near enough to eject the Sun from the galaxy, where the escape speed at the Sun's position is more than five hundred kilometres per second. So the Sun carries on, a little lonelier, still shining.

Now stay with me, because the next part is the surprise inside the disaster. What happens to a planet in the dark?

The black hole is behind us, and its pull weakens with the square of distance. After one year, it is about six astronomical units away. After a decade, about sixty three. After about sixteen years, it is well beyond Neptune and heading out into the dark, a sixty kilometre ball of nothing carrying ten Suns of mass.

Follow Earth, flung out at, say, thirty kilometres per second, about the speed it used to orbit. After one year it is about six astronomical units farther out, and sunlight is less than a fiftieth of what it was. After a decade, at roughly sixty four astronomical units, the Sun is a single point of light. It is still about a hundred times brighter than the full Moon, but it gives almost no warmth. Photosynthesis stops. The land cools fastest. The oceans, which hold far more heat, cool far more slowly. The average ocean is about three point seven kilometres deep, and its deep water stays warm for a very long time.

What happens next depends on a number. Earth's interior still leaks heat, about forty seven trillion watts, according to a widely used estimate. That sounds enormous. But it is less than a thousandth of what sunlight used to deliver. Do a back of the envelope calculation, with Earth radiating that heat into empty space, and the surface settles near thirty five kelvin, which is about two hundred and thirty eight degrees below zero Celsius. That is far colder than the point where nitrogen freezes, about sixty three kelvin. So the air itself eventually falls as snow. If you spread it evenly, the frozen atmosphere would lie roughly ten metres deep across the entire planet.

How long that takes is uncertain. The answer depends on the details of the atmosphere and the ice, and different models disagree. But the end state is a world sealed under a shell of frozen air and ice, with no daylight at all.

Here is the strange part. It is not necessarily dead.

In 1977, scientists exploring the Galápagos Rift in a small submarine found something nobody expected. On the dark seafloor, around hot vents, there were thick colonies of tubeworms, clams, and crabs. They did not run on sunlight. They ran on chemical energy from the Earth itself, with bacteria turning vent chemicals into food. Since then, we have found microbes living in coal beds about two and a half kilometres below the seafloor. Earth's interior stays hot for far longer than any human timescale, and that heat is all these communities need.

So here is the fair version. The forests, the farms, the cities, everything that depends on sunlight and open air, is gone within years. That part is not in doubt. But the deep biosphere that lives on rock and chemistry does not obviously notice. Whether it lasts a million years or a billion depends on whether liquid water survives in the crust. If the ice grows thicker than the oceans are deep, the oceans may freeze solid, and liquid water might survive only in pores deep underground. Nobody knows the answer, and the honest range is wide.

One more thing happens, much later. Remember the Oort cloud, the distant shell of icy bodies around the Sun, perhaps a trillion of them, reaching out to perhaps a hundred thousand astronomical units. At thirty kilometres per second, the black hole spends about sixteen thousand years crossing that region before it reaches the planets. Every icy body it passes gets nudged, and some are knocked inward. But falling from fifty thousand astronomical units takes about two million years. So the comet shower from this encounter arrives long after everyone in this story is gone, and long after the black hole is somewhere else entirely.

And here is where the story turns.

Everything so far assumed the black hole is an intruder, something that drifts in from outside, does damage, and leaves. But there is a serious, unproven, and contested idea that the solar system may already contain a black hole, and that it has been here the whole time.

In 2016, the astronomers Konstantin Batygin and Mike Brown proposed that an undiscovered planet, perhaps ten times the mass of Earth, orbiting hundreds of astronomical units from the Sun, could explain why a handful of distant icy bodies have orbits that cluster in an odd way. They called it Planet Nine. Nobody has found it. Some analyses argue the clustering is just a trick of where telescopes have happened to look.

Then in 2019, two physicists, Jakub Scholtz and James Unwin, asked a stranger question. What if the thing out there is not a planet at all? What if it is a primordial black hole? That is a black hole that formed in the first moments of the universe, long before any star, rather than from a dying one. Primordial black holes are hypothetical. No one has confirmed that any exist. But if one had about five times the mass of Earth, its gravity would pull on those distant icy objects exactly as a planet would. And its radius would be only about four and a half centimetres. A sphere under ten centimetres across, with the pull of five worlds.

That idea was partly inspired by a handful of odd, very short microlensing events, seen by a survey called OGLE, which hint at Earth mass objects with no star. Whether those events are primordial black holes, rogue planets, or something else is still argued over.

So hold the two stories together. A passing black hole of ten Suns would wreck the solar system. A hidden black hole of five Earths, sitting hundreds of astronomical units out, would do nothing to us at all. It would be the quietest neighbour imaginable. The only difference between the catastrophe and the harmless ghost is mass and distance. Not the hole itself.

And there is a way to start finding out. The Vera Rubin Observatory is built for a ten year survey of the whole southern sky, and one thing it can do is search for faint, slow moving objects in the outer solar system, exactly where Planet Nine would be. If something shows up, we learn whether it is a planet. If nothing shows up, the clustering may be an illusion. And if the gravity is there but nothing can be seen, then we will have to follow the gravity alone.

That brings us back to the field at night, and the stars that have always been where they are. We tend to think that if something enormous were coming, we would see it. But a black hole at thirty kilometres per second covers one light year in roughly ten thousand years. That is a very long warning, and a very easy one to miss. The first isolated black hole ever detected, by microlensing in 2022, still has competing mass estimates from different teams.
