Almost two thousand nine hundred kilometres beneath your boots, in total darkness, a ball of metal that has been blazing hot for four and a half billion years goes cold. You are standing on a quiet street at dawn, coffee in one hand, phone in the other. The cooling is not slow. It happens all at once. Nothing shakes. No siren sounds. The sun keeps rising and your coffee keeps steaming. And yet the most important machine on this planet has just lost its fuel, a machine you have never seen, never felt, and have relied on every second of your life.

Before we go any further, let us be plain about the premise. This cannot happen. Heat does not leave the centre of a planet in an instant. It has to climb through almost three thousand kilometres of rock, and that climb takes billions of years. The real core is cooling right now, but so slowly that you will never notice. So for this video we break one rule, once. We say the core's heat simply vanishes. Then we leave every other law of physics switched on, and we watch what follows.

What follows is not what the movies promised. The thing most people expect to hurt us turns out to be the wrong thing to fear, and the thing almost nobody thinks about turns out to matter most. We will get there. But first you need to know what has just stopped.

The core holds about a third of the planet's mass. Its radius is a little over half of Earth's, which makes it roughly sixteen percent of the planet's volume. It comes in two parts. At the very centre sits the inner core, a solid ball of iron and nickel with a radius of about twelve hundred and twenty kilometres. That is around seventy percent of the radius of the Moon. Around it sits the outer core, a shell of liquid metal about twenty-two hundred and sixty kilometres thick, churning beneath the mantle.

Nobody has ever been there, so the temperatures are a careful reconstruction, and the honest answer is that they are still argued over. The best experiments squeeze iron between diamonds, heat it with lasers, and put the edge of the inner core at about six thousand kelvin, give or take several hundred. That is comparable to the surface of the Sun. The pressure at the very centre is about three hundred and sixty gigapascals, more than three million times the pressure of the air around you right now. The inner core stays solid not because it is cool, but because it is squeezed too hard to melt.

Why does any of this matter to you? Because the liquid part of that core is a dynamo. Liquid iron conducts electricity extremely well. When it churns inside a spinning planet, it drives electric currents, and those currents generate a magnetic field that reaches through the mantle and far out into space. Churning needs energy. Two sources are usually named. One is heat leaking upward out of the core. The other is the inner core itself, which grows as liquid freezes onto its surface, by about a millimetre a year in most models. Each frozen layer squeezes lighter elements out into the liquid and stirs it.

How old the inner core is, scientists do not agree. Estimates run from about half a billion years to a bit over a billion. Yet magnetised rocks show that Earth already had a magnetic field about three and a half billion years ago. So a dynamo can run without an inner core. It only needs a steady supply of energy. In our scenario, that supply has just been cut. Let us take the timeline in order, starting with the first second.

In the first second, you feel nothing. Gravity does not change, because the core has lost heat, not mass. Your bathroom scale reads the same. The ground holds still. But far below, a layer of liquid metal has turned solid, and freezing metal does something that matters.

It shrinks. Solid iron under those pressures takes up less room than liquid iron, so the mantle above has to settle inward to fill the gap. Rock does this slowly, flowing like extremely stiff honey over centuries and millennia. So in the first hours you would see no change at all. How far the surface would eventually sink depends on the exact recipe of the core's alloy, which is still debated. I will not give you a number, because I would have to invent one. I will only say that the shape of the whole planet starts, very quietly, to change.

Within minutes, the first people to notice are seismologists. Earthquakes send two kinds of wave through the Earth. Pressure waves push and pull like sound and travel through almost anything. Shear waves shake sideways and cannot cross a liquid. In 1906 the geologist Richard Oldham spotted that earthquake waves slowed down after passing through the planet's middle, which revealed a distinct core. In 1926 the physicist Harold Jeffreys showed that the core must be liquid, because shear waves do not cross it. Then in 1936 the seismologist Inge Lehmann found faint pressure waves bouncing off something inside it, and that is how we learned there was a solid inner core.

Now those old clues reverse. A shear wave from a large earthquake can cross what used to be the liquid outer core and reach the far side of the planet, where such waves do not normally appear. A pressure wave takes roughly twenty minutes to cross the whole Earth to the opposite point, and its arrival time would shift. Seismometers across the world would record a pattern that no textbook describes. That would be the headline. But the more interesting question is what your compass is doing, and the answer is going to surprise you.

Hours pass. The day goes on. There is one more place the change would show up. The liquid core exchanges spin with the mantle, and that exchange is thought to be part of the reason the length of the day wanders by roughly a millisecond over the course of decades. Lock the core to the mantle and that wandering would change character. Only atomic clocks and radio astronomers would notice. Everyone else is still on their way to work.

Now it is the afternoon of the first day. You take out a compass. The needle swings to the north, exactly as it always has. You open a space weather site, and the aurora is still glowing over Norway and Alaska tonight. The field is still there. Nothing you can measure has changed.

This is the first surprise, because almost everyone assumes that when the dynamo stops, the shield drops. It does not. A magnetic field threaded through a good electrical conductor cannot just switch off. To fade, it has to leak away, the way heat leaks out of a hot stone. The slowest-fading, broadest part of the field, the dipole that makes your compass point north, has a leak time of roughly twenty thousand years. That figure depends on how well iron conducts electricity at core pressures, which mineral physicists still argue about. Some estimates run shorter. Some run a good deal longer. None of them say days.

So after a week, nothing. After a month, nothing. Earth has become a bell that has been struck and has not yet gone quiet. Keep that picture in mind, because it rewrites the whole timeline. The disaster we usually imagine is off by tens of thousands of years. Stay with me through the years, because the first thing to change is not the strength of the field. It is something much stranger.

In the first years the compass still works, but its behaviour changes. Today the magnetic north pole wanders. In recent decades it has raced across the Arctic at tens of kilometres a year, heading from Canada toward Siberia. That wandering is steered by the flow of liquid metal deep down. Stop the flow and the steering stops. The pole would slow, then settle. Navigators would be handed the strangest gift in their history, a magnetic map that stops going out of date.

The shape of the field would also begin to simplify. Small, fine-grained features leak away fastest, and the broad dipole lasts longest. So the lumpy, complicated field of today slowly smooths into something close to a simple bar magnet, only weaker.

And here is a detail that should bother you. In the real world, the field has weakened by about nine percent over the last two hundred years, according to the European Space Agency's Swarm satellites. On our dead Earth, with a leak time of twenty thousand years, the field would lose only about one percent over the same two centuries. Think about that. The planet with a stopped core would, for thousands of years, fade more gently than the planet we actually live on. The real decline is driven by moving metal and shifting patches of reversed magnetic flux, and most researchers see it as part of normal fluctuation, not necessarily the start of a reversal.

Now stretch the clock. If the leak time is twenty thousand years, then after a thousand years the field has lost about five percent. After ten thousand years it is down to about sixty percent of today's. After twenty thousand, about a third. After forty thousand, roughly a seventh. These are clean numbers from a simplified model, and the real curve would wobble, but the shape is right. This is not a collapse. It is a very long goodbye. And somewhere along that curve, things start to matter. The first to notice will not be us.

Think of everything alive that reads the field. Sea turtles, migratory birds, salmon, and many insects appear to use it as a compass or a map. Loggerhead turtle hatchlings, tested in tanks in the laboratory of Kenneth Lohmann, change their swimming direction when the field around them is rotated. How the sensing works is still contested. One idea involves light-sensitive proteins called cryptochromes, in which the field changes the chemistry of pairs of electrons. Another involves tiny crystals of magnetite. Possibly both. What we know is that the field these animals rely on is, at the surface, between about twenty-five and sixty-five microtesla, and that in our scenario it would fade over thousands of generations. That is slow enough for evolution to respond, and evolution has had practice, as we will see in a moment.

The sky would change too, in a way you might enjoy at first. The aurora is drawn into a ring around each magnetic pole. As the field weakens, that ring would creep toward the equator, as it briefly did in the great storm of 1859, when aurora was reported in the Caribbean. Postcards from Florida would show green curtains. It would feel like a gift, right up until you ask what a weaker shield is letting through.

What about the ground under your feet? You might think a cold core would switch off the volcanoes. It would not, at least not quickly. Earth loses about forty-six terawatts of heat through its surface. The core is thought to supply only a fraction of that, somewhere between about five and fifteen terawatts, and the range is itself debated. The rest comes from the mantle, from radioactive elements and from the leftover heat of the planet's formation. So plate tectonics carries on. What might fade, over millions of years, are the great plumes that some geologists believe rise from the base of the mantle to feed hotspots like Hawaii and Iceland. Even that is contested, because other researchers think those hotspots come from much shallower sources.

Now the technology. The best-known modern warning is the storm of March thirteenth, 1989, when disturbed magnetic fields drove currents through the long power lines of Quebec and the grid collapsed in about ninety seconds, leaving some six million people without power for roughly nine hours. A planet with a failing field sounds like a planet doomed to blackouts. The truth is that nobody knows. The storms that hurt grids come from the way the magnetosphere reacts to the solar wind, and a planet with almost no magnetosphere would react in a different way, one that has never been measured on a world with oceans and a conducting crust like ours.

Satellites are a clearer case. In 1958 Explorer One discovered the radiation belts, rings of charged particles trapped by the field. They would shrink and weaken. But the bubble the field carves out of the solar wind shrinks too. In the standard picture, the size of that bubble scales as the cube root of the field strength. Today it reaches about ten Earth radii on the sunward side. When the field is down to a seventh of today's, that falls to about five. Geostationary satellites orbit at about six and a half Earth radii from the centre of the planet. By then, any satellite parked there would sit outside the bubble, in the raw solar wind, much of the time. That is a problem, but notice it is a problem for machines, tens of thousands of years from now.

For people, the better question is how bad a very weak field really is, and we do not have to guess, because the planet has already run the experiment. Every few hundred thousand years, on average, though very irregularly, the field reverses, and north and south swap. The last full reversal was about seven hundred and seventy thousand years ago. During reversals the field does not vanish, but it drops, often to a small fraction of its normal strength, for thousands of years. Our own species lived through a sharp dip about forty-one thousand years ago. It is called the Laschamp event, after lava flows in central France, and the field then fell to roughly five percent of today's.

Here the science gets contested. In 2021 an international team including Alan Cooper and Chris Turney published a paper in Science arguing that the lowest point of that dip, which they called the Adams Event, around forty-two thousand years ago, coincided with a global environmental crisis, with climate shifts, the loss of giant animals in Australia, and even changes in how our ancestors lived. Their proposed cause was ozone loss and charged particles. Many scientists are not convinced. They point out that the dating of extinctions is uncertain and that the link between cause and effect is unproven. Both things are true. The field was nearly gone, and we are here.

Now the fear you came in with. If the field fades, do we get cooked? The air above you is the real armour. The column of air over each square centimetre of ground weighs about one kilogram, which stops radiation about as well as ten metres of water. Cosmic rays smash into that air and break apart high above your head. At the ground, they give a typical person about four tenths of a millisievert a year, out of roughly two point four from all natural sources, according to the United Nations Scientific Committee on the Effects of Atomic Radiation. Take away the field and that number climbs, most of all near the equator, where the field currently turns away the most particles. But the atmosphere is still there, and reviews of past field collapses find the rise at ground level modest. The bigger worry is ozone. Energetic particles that reach the upper atmosphere make nitrogen oxides, and those destroy ozone. Models disagree on how much, and evidence from past events is thin.

Which leaves the great fear, the one in every documentary. Without a magnetic field, the solar wind strips the sky. The solar wind blows past us at typically around four hundred kilometres per second. Does it take our air? Look at Mars. It lost its global field roughly four billion years ago. Today its atmosphere is thin, with a surface pressure under one percent of Earth's. NASA's MAVEN spacecraft measured the solar wind stripping gas from the top of that atmosphere at about a hundred grams every second. Case closed? Not quite.

Mars also keeps a ghost of its dynamo. In the late 1990s, Mars Global Surveyor found patches of the Martian crust that are strongly magnetised, fossils of the field that once ran there. Earth's rocks keep fossils too, locked in whenever magnetite cools below about five hundred and eighty degrees Celsius. On a dead Earth, those would be the last traces of our field, faint and patchy, written in stone for anyone who came to look.

Venus has no global field either, yet it holds an atmosphere with a surface pressure about ninety times ours. Venus is bigger and heavier than Mars, so it grips its gas better, and that is part of the story. But there is more. A 2018 study led by Herbert Gunell compared Earth, Mars and Venus and found that the rates at which they lose ions to space are broadly similar, even though one has a strong field and the other two have none. Earth's field, it turns out, can also funnel energy from the solar wind into the polar upper atmosphere and help drag ions out. Even now, with the shield up, Earth leaks about three kilograms of hydrogen and fifty grams of helium every second. Other researchers argue that over billions of years a field still helps a planet keep its water. Both camps have data. Nobody has settled it.

So the sky may well survive the loss of the field. Which brings us to the last turn, and it is the one that stopped me when I first read it. The thing that eventually takes our air is probably not the core at all. As the Sun slowly brightens, Earth's oxygen-rich atmosphere is expected to end in about a billion years. A 2021 study in Nature Geoscience by Kazumi Ozaki and Christopher Reinhard put the figure at about one point oh eight billion years, give or take a little. When the real core will freeze and the real dynamo will stop, nobody can say with confidence, because it hangs on properties of iron that we cannot yet measure well. Some models give the dynamo a very long life. Others do not.

So the scenario we just walked through is not a fantasy. It is a rehearsal. Mars shows us what a dead core can do to a small planet over four billion years. Venus shows us that a dead core is not the same as a dead sky. And Earth, for all its violent interior, may meet its end from above, not from below. The compass fades, the aurora creeps toward the tropics, the turtles adapt or they do not. And the air, most likely, stays.

So here is what I want to know. If the field faded over tens of thousands of years, and you could keep only one of its gifts, the compass, the aurora, or the shield, which would you save? And do you think the sky would hold? Tell me in the comments.
