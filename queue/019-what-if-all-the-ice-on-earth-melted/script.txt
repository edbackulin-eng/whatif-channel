Stand on a beach at dawn. The sand is cold under your feet, the tide is out, and the sea sits exactly where it has sat for your whole life. Then, in a single instant, every piece of ice on Earth turns to water. The ice sheet that buries Antarctica. The one that covers Greenland. Every glacier in the Alps, the Andes and the Himalaya. All of it, gone between one heartbeat and the next.

Look at the waterline. Nothing moves. And that quiet is the most deceptive part of this story, because what is coming for that beach is taller than a twenty story building.

First, let us be honest about the premise. It is impossible. Melting that much ice takes energy, and the amount is staggering. Ice needs about 334 kilojoules to melt each kilogram, and that is before you spend a single joule warming it up to freezing. The ice on Earth adds up to roughly 30 million cubic kilometers. Do the multiplication and you need around nine trillion trillion joules. The Sun delivers about 170 quadrillion watts to the planet. At that rate, melting all the ice would swallow every ray of sunlight that strikes Earth for a year and a half. Nothing on this planet can do that in an instant, or in a century.

So we will grant the cheat once. The ice becomes liquid water, at freezing temperature, right where it sits. After that, we switch the physics back on, and we follow it wherever it goes. Stay with me, because the first thing that happens is not the flood. It is something much stranger, and it starts in the very first second.

Begin in Antarctica. The ice there is about two kilometers thick on average, and more than four kilometers thick in places. Antarctica holds around ninety percent of all the ice on the planet. For a few seconds, that continent is a lake the size of a continent, two kilometers deep, sitting on rock that has been buried for millions of years.

Water does not stay stacked. Gravity pulls it flat, and it does so fast. Here is a simple piece of physics. A long, shallow wave in water travels at a speed equal to the square root of gravity times the depth. For a depth of two kilometers, that comes to about 140 meters per second. That is roughly 500 kilometers an hour, as an upper limit. Rough ground, rock and friction would slow it, and as the water drains the speed drops. But the front of that surge would still move far faster than anything on the surface of Earth normally does.

Now ask what the water feels. It sits at zero degrees. The air above it is a different story. The coldest air temperature ever measured on Earth was minus 89.2 degrees Celsius, at Vostok Station in Antarctica. So the top of that vast lake would begin freezing at once, forming a skin of new ice over a deep basin of liquid. A skin is all it would get. A lake two kilometers deep takes a very long time to freeze through, and the water underneath is already rushing toward the coast.

In a moment we will follow that surge into the ocean, and what it meets there is not what you would expect. But first, one more thing happens in these opening seconds, and it makes this scenario different from any flood in history.

The land is suddenly lighter. For millions of years, the bedrock under Antarctica and Greenland has been pressed down by the weight of the ice. That crust rides on the hot, slowly flowing rock of the mantle, and it has been sagging into it. The moment the ice becomes water and runs off, the load vanishes. The crust cannot spring back in seconds. Rock does not move like that. It will take thousands of years. But the push upward begins now, and we will come back to it, because it leads to the most surprising consequence of all.

Now the minutes. The surge reaches the Antarctic coast and meets the Southern Ocean. Here is a fact that changes the picture. Fresh water is lighter than seawater. Seawater has a density of about 1,025 kilograms per cubic meter, and fresh water about 1,000. So the meltwater does not dive in and mix. It rides on top. A huge sheet of fresh water spreads across the surface of the Southern Ocean, like oil on a puddle, though far less tidy. Waves and storms will stir it down over weeks and months, not minutes.

At the same time, the ocean itself reacts. When a large volume of water is added to the sea, the extra height does not stay near the source. It travels as a wave. The average ocean depth is about 3,700 meters, and a long wave in water that deep travels at roughly 700 kilometers an hour, close to the speed of a jet airliner. This is the same physics as a tsunami in the open sea. So the news of the new water races out from the pole in every direction.

Here is the part nobody expects. The sea level does not rise evenly. Ice is heavy, and heavy things have gravity. A giant ice sheet pulls the surrounding ocean toward itself, piling water up against its coast. Remove the ice, and the pull goes with it. Scientists worked this out in detail around the turn of the century. The result is that near a melting ice sheet, the sea level can actually fall, while far from it, the rise is bigger than the global average. Melt Greenland, and the water near Greenland drops while the water near the tropics rises more than you would expect. Keep that in mind, because it changes which coasts take the worst of it.

Next, the hours. The surge has left the pole and the ocean is rearranging itself. The ice that already floats on the sea is a puzzle worth a moment. Floating ice has already pushed aside its own weight in water. When it melts, the level barely changes. This is Archimedes, and it is why sea ice and floating ice shelves add very little to the rise. What matters is the ice sitting on land. That is Antarctica, Greenland, and the glaciers on mountains.

So how much water is that? Researchers who mapped the rock beneath the ice put the numbers together. The Antarctic ice sheet holds enough to raise the sea by about 58 meters. Greenland holds about 7 meters. All the mountain glaciers together hold roughly a third of a meter. The total comes to about 66 meters, and the United States Geological Survey rounds it to about 70. You will see both figures. They are not in conflict. They come from slightly different ways of counting.

If you simply divide the water by the area of today's oceans, you get an answer a bit over 70 meters. The real answer is smaller for two reasons. As the sea climbs, it spills over land, and the ocean gets wider, so the same water makes a smaller rise. And the added weight of the water pushes the seafloor down a little. Both effects shave off some of the height.

Hold on to that number. In a moment we will put it on a map. It is much more than a flood. It redraws the planet.

Now the days. The water has had time to spread, and the new shoreline is racing inland. Because the ocean adjusts at wave speed, the global surface evens out in a matter of days, apart from the gravity effects we just saw. What does 70 meters mean for the places where people actually live?

About four in ten people on Earth live within 100 kilometers of a coast. More than 600 million live on land less than ten meters above the sea. Those places go first. The Netherlands, where about a quarter of the country already sits below sea level, becomes seabed. Most of Bangladesh, one of the most crowded countries on Earth, disappears. The great river deltas, the Nile, the Mekong, the Ganges, the Yangtze, all go under.

At 70 meters, the great port cities go too. London, New York, Shanghai, Mumbai, Lagos and Tokyo would be flooded or cut off. Tall buildings might still stand in places, with their upper floors above the water, but the streets, the subways, the power plants and the harbors would not. Florida would be reduced to little more than a thin strip of land in the north, where Britton Hill, the highest point in the state at 105 meters, would remain above the sea.

And remember, in our scenario this is not a slow retreat. There is no warning, no evacuation, and no time. The people nearest the water have hours. The ones who live through it are the ones who happen to live on high ground, and they now face a world where nearly every port, every coastal power station and a large part of the farmland is gone.

There is something in the next stage that scientists argue about, and it matters for how the following years unfold. It is about the ocean itself.

Now, the weeks and months. The ocean is no longer the same ocean. About two percent more water has been added to it, all of it fresh. That sounds small. It is not. Seawater is about 3.5 percent salt. Adding two percent of fresh water dilutes the whole ocean by about two percent, and far more near the surface, where the meltwater lies in a thick layer.

Why does salt matter? Because ocean currents are driven in part by density. In the North Atlantic, cold, salty water sinks and flows south through the deep ocean, a process called the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation. This conveyor carries heat north and helps keep Western Europe milder than other places at the same latitude. Fresh water on the surface is lighter, and it resists sinking. If it stops the sinking, the conveyor slows or stops.

Here we have to be careful, because this is genuinely contested. Some studies suggest the circulation could collapse within decades under enough freshwater input. Others, including the assessment of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, say an abrupt collapse before the year 2100 is unlikely, though they have only low confidence about what happens after that. Some researchers link a cold snap about 12,900 years ago, called the Younger Dryas, to a surge of meltwater into the Atlantic, but even that explanation is debated. So the honest answer is that nobody knows exactly what would happen to the conveyor in this scenario.

At the same time, another change arrives. Ice covers about ten percent of the land on Earth. It is white, and white things reflect light. Fresh snow bounces back eighty or ninety percent of the sunlight that hits it. The open ocean reflects only about six percent. Replace ice with water and bare rock, and the planet starts absorbing more of the Sun's energy. The warming feeds on itself.

That is the short term. To understand the long term, we have to ask a better question. How would this happen in the real world, where nothing happens in an instant?

Now the years, and the real timeline. In the real world, ice does not vanish in a day. It goes slowly, and the speed depends on how warm we make the planet. According to NASA's GRACE satellites, Greenland has been losing about 270 billion tonnes of ice per year on average since 2002, and Antarctica about 150 billion tonnes a year. That sounds enormous, and it is. Yet Greenland holds more than two quadrillion tonnes. At that pace, the Greenland ice sheet would take about ten thousand years to melt. The pace is not constant, and it has been rising. But it shows how slowly even a fast melt moves on the scale of a whole ice sheet.

Is there a point of no return? One widely cited study of Greenland found that the ice sheet could enter runaway loss somewhere between 0.8 and 3.2 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial temperatures, with a best guess of 1.6. That range is wide, and that width is the honest state of the science. For Antarctica, a study in 2015 found that burning all the fossil fuels we can reach could eliminate the whole ice sheet, over the following millennia. The key word is millennia.

Then there are the places that make scientists most nervous. Thwaites Glacier in West Antarctica is about the size of Florida. By itself it holds enough ice to raise the sea by about 65 centimeters, and it acts as a cork holding back more ice behind it. Its nickname, the Doomsday Glacier, is dramatic and a little unfair, but the concern behind it is real. It is already retreating.

What does the past tell us? About 125,000 years ago, during the last warm period between ice ages, the sea stood between five and ten meters higher than today. Where that water came from, Greenland, Antarctica or both, is still argued. About three million years ago, in the mid-Pliocene, carbon dioxide was close to today's levels, and the sea stood well above its present height. Estimates range from several meters to well over twenty, and researchers disagree about how high it really got. So while nobody expects all the ice to go, even a small part of it would redraw the coasts.

Now the thousands of years. Suppose the melting runs all the way to the end. What does an ice-free Earth look like? The planet has been there before. Before about 34 million years ago, when the Antarctic ice sheet first formed, the world had no permanent ice sheets at all.

About 55 million years ago, during a hot spike called the Paleocene Eocene Thermal Maximum, drill cores from the Arctic Ocean show surface water around 23 degrees Celsius. That is a warm swimming temperature, near the North Pole. In the warm Eocene that followed, the far north held swamp forests and relatives of alligators, on Ellesmere Island, deep inside the Arctic Circle. The carbon dioxide level then was much higher than now, though the exact figure is uncertain and still argued. Today it sits above 420 parts per million.

The oceans, after all that melting, would take about a thousand years to turn over and mix fully, so the effects roll on for centuries after the water arrives. Coral reefs would drown or move. Fisheries would rearrange themselves. Old farmland would sink beneath the waves. But there is one consequence we have not reached yet. It is the one almost nobody expects, and it comes from the planet itself.

Go back to that crust, lightened by the loss of the ice. Remember that the land starts to rise the moment the weight is gone. This is not theoretical. In parts of Scandinavia, the land is still rising after the last ice age ended, at close to a centimeter a year. And in West Antarctica, where ice is thinning today, scientists measured the bedrock rising by up to 41 millimeters a year, one of the fastest rebounds ever recorded. The mantle under West Antarctica is unusually soft and hot, so it responds faster than most.

Now think about what happens to a volcano when the weight on top of it is removed. Pressure helps keep magma in place. Take the pressure away, and melt in the rock can rise more easily. Scientists have seen this in Iceland. After the glaciers retreated about 12,000 years ago, eruption rates there were somewhere between 30 and 50 times higher than they are now, for a period of a few thousand years. A study of volcanic records around the world found that global eruptions were two to six times more frequent in the millennia after the last ice age than before it.

Here is where this scenario turns strange. In 2017, a team at the University of Edinburgh found 91 previously unknown volcanoes under the West Antarctic ice, bringing the total to 138 in that region. They described it as one of the densest volcanic regions on Earth, and almost all of it has been pressed down under ice. Remove that ice, and the weight comes off an entire volcanic province.

Does that mean a wave of eruptions? Scientists disagree. Some think the ice acts as a lid, and that as it thins, eruptions become more likely. Others say the effect is real in the long run but slow, playing out over centuries to millennia, and that most of those volcanoes might simply stay quiet. Nobody has watched it happen under a continent. Nobody can say for certain.

But consider the full picture. You have a planet whose oceans have risen by about 70 meters, whose coasts have been redrawn, and whose ice no longer reflects the Sun. The crust is rising where the ice has gone, and the seafloor is sinking under its new load. That shift of mass is large enough to measure from space. It is already moving the position of the pole, and the ice melt that has already happened has delayed the next negative leap second by about three years, according to a 2024 study. A world with no ice does not just have a different map. It has a different shape, and a slightly different spin.

So go back to the beach where you began. The shore you stood on is long gone, buried below the new waves. The coasts have moved far inland, and in the far south the ground is quietly rising, one millimeter after another, on a continent that has not seen bare earth in millions of years.

None of this is a prediction, and none of it would happen all at once. But every piece of it, the rising seas, the shifting gravity, the freshening ocean, the rebounding crust and the waking volcanoes, is physics that is already at work today, just on a much smaller scale.
