Your boots are standing in the grey dust of the Sea of Tranquility. Ahead of you is a bootprint, sharp as a stamp, left there more than half a century ago. Above you the sky is black, and the Sun is a hard white coin that does not glow or twinkle. There is no sound. There has never been any sound here, except what travels up through your own bones and the metal of your suit.

Now something changes. A faint hiss starts at the edge of hearing. The black above you begins to turn deep blue, climbing from the horizon like ink running backward. The hiss becomes a roar. And then your suit, which has kept you alive for hours, starts to squeeze you.

For the next few minutes, you are going to live inside one strange idea: the Moon, wrapped in air as thick as ours. Keep that bootprint in mind. By the end, we will find out what happens to it. And we will find one thing this air would let you do on the Moon that you cannot do anywhere on Earth, a thing that surprised me when I worked through the numbers.

Here is the honest starting point. The Moon cannot have an atmosphere like this. Not for long, and not without a miracle. No law of nature forbids the idea. The problem is that nothing in the solar system is going to deliver the gas for free, and the Moon would not hold on to it if it did. So this is a thought experiment with the physics left switched on. We give the Moon an atmosphere with the same mix as ours: about seventy eight percent nitrogen, twenty one percent oxygen, and a little under one percent argon. Then we watch gravity, sunlight and the behaviour of gases do their work.

First, how much air is that? The Moon is about three and a half thousand kilometres across. Its surface gravity is one point six two metres per second squared, about one sixth of ours. Pressure at the bottom of an atmosphere is just the weight of the air stacked above you. Weaker gravity means each kilogram of air weighs less, so you need more kilograms overhead to get the same pressure.

To match the pressure we feel at sea level, about one hundred and one thousand pascals, you would need roughly two point four quintillion kilograms of gas. That is a little under half the mass of the entire atmosphere of Earth, which is about five point one quintillion kilograms. Why less than half, when the gravity is so weak? Because the Moon has only about one thirteenth of the surface area of Earth. There is far less ground to cover, and that wins out over the weak gravity.

The shape of that air would be different, though. On Earth, the atmosphere thins out with a characteristic height of about eight and a half kilometres. On the Moon, with a sixth of the gravity, the same air would puff up about six times higher, to a characteristic height near fifty kilometres. It would be a bloated, towering sky. And here is a consequence you can feel in your chest. Above each square metre of ground, there would be about six times as many air molecules as above each square metre of Earth. Hold on to that number, because it matters for almost everything that follows.

Now let us count the first seconds.

In the first second, the air arrives. The pressure on your suit climbs from almost nothing to a full atmosphere. How close to nothing? The air that is on the Moon right now is more than a hundred trillion times thinner than the air you are breathing, a value measured by an instrument that Apollo 17 left behind. We are changing that by fourteen orders of magnitude, in an instant.

Your suit feels it first. The Apollo suits ran at about three point seven pounds per square inch of pure oxygen. The air outside is now fourteen point seven. For the first time in your life in this suit, the outside is pushing in about four times harder than the inside pushes out. The fabric stops ballooning and starts to clamp. Joints that astronauts fought to bend now fight you in a different way.

Within ten seconds, you do something that sounds reckless and turns out fine. You unlatch your helmet. The air is breathable. Twenty one percent oxygen at one atmosphere, at a pleasant fifteen degrees, which is the standard temperature we use for sea level air. You breathe in, and the smell of the Moon reaches you for the first time. Astronauts described it as spent gunpowder, because the dust, once it got inside the cabin, carried that smell.

And now there is sound. In air at fifteen degrees, sound travels at about three hundred and forty metres per second. Your footsteps are no longer silent. Your voice leaves your mouth and reaches your own ears, instead of going through a radio. For a moment you just stand there, talking to yourself, because on this world nobody has ever been able to do that.

But the sky is what will stop you in your tracks. It is changing colour, and it is not changing the way you expect. Stay with me for the next few minutes, because the Sun is about to do something strange.

Within minutes, the sky settles into a colour. Air scatters short wavelengths of light far more strongly than long ones, which is why our sky is blue and our sunsets are red. On the Moon, you would see the same effect, but with six times as many molecules overhead. The blue would be brighter and deeper, bright enough to wipe the stars from the daytime sky, just as it does on Earth. The Sun would hang in it, no longer a hard coin on black velvet, but a softened disc with a faint glow around it.

And there is something else in that sky. The Moon always shows the same face to Earth, so from the near side, Earth hangs in one place, almost motionless, for the whole month. It would look almost four times wider than the Moon looks from here. In a blue daytime sky it would be pale, a washed out disc, like our own Moon on a sunny afternoon. At night, though, it would blaze, and it would be the brightest thing in the sky, a swirl of white cloud and dark ocean that never sets.

Sunsets would be strange as well. The Sun sets slowly here, because the Moon turns once in a little over twenty nine days. Light grazing the horizon would pass through a vast length of air, and the sky would burn red and orange for hours.

Now, a small test. In nineteen seventy one, on Apollo fifteen, David Scott held out a hammer in one hand and a falcon feather in the other and let go. They landed together, because without air, everything falls at the same rate. On our air-wrapped Moon, run it again. The hammer still drops like a stone. But the feather drifts. It flutters and wobbles and takes its time. Because gravity is weaker, the feather would settle at only about two fifths of the speed it falls at on Earth, so it would float down almost lazily. A feather on the Moon, with air, would be a more graceful thing than it is here.

That is a pleasant picture. But the Sun is climbing, and the ground it climbs over is about to cause trouble. Let us see what happens over the next several hours.

The Sun moves slowly across the lunar sky. Because a lunar day is about twenty nine and a half Earth days long, it creeps along at about half a degree per hour. From sunrise to local noon takes a little over a week. So over the hours after the air arrives, you would see the ground warming, minute by minute, with a slowness that has no equivalent on Earth.

Today, with no air, the equator at lunar noon reaches about one hundred and twenty seven degrees Celsius. The dust is a superb insulator, so only the surface skin gets hot. But now there is air on top of it. Air touching a hot surface warms, becomes lighter, and rises. Columns of warm air start to climb in the afternoon, and cooler air flows in beneath them. That is the beginning of wind.

Wind on the Moon behaves in a way that would surprise a meteorologist. Because gravity is weak, the dust grains weigh only a sixth as much as the same grains would on Earth. The wind speed needed to lift a grain scales with the square root of gravity. So a breeze that does nothing on Earth could pick up the lunar dust and carry it. The Moon, in the afternoon, would be a dusty place.

That matters, because lunar dust is nasty stuff. It has never been worn smooth by wind and water, so the grains are sharp and jagged. Harrison Schmitt, on Apollo seventeen, developed what he called lunar hay fever after the dust got into the cabin. Now imagine your helmet off, and that dust blowing around you in a breeze. Breathing the Moon would be a bad idea. You would want to put the helmet back on, or at least a mask.

Now think about the sky above you. Right now, nothing slows the rocks that fall onto the Moon. Meteoroids hit the surface at full speed, and every one makes a crater, or at least a pit. With six times as much air above each square metre as we have on Earth, the Moon would have a much better shield than we do. On Earth, estimates of how much cosmic dust and rock enters our atmosphere each day range from about five to three hundred tonnes. That range is contested, because the measurements are hard. Almost all of it burns up high above us.

On the Moon, the same thing would happen, but better. Small meteoroids would flare and vanish. At night you would see shooting stars, which no astronaut has ever seen from the lunar surface. Larger rocks would still get through. The object that exploded over Chelyabinsk in two thousand and thirteen was about twenty metres wide. It broke windows across a city, and it did not leave a crater. Something that size would be a crater maker on the airless Moon. With air, it might break up and burst overhead instead.

So over the long run, the Moon would stop collecting small craters. The great scarred surface we know would, slowly, stop getting scarred. But first, there is a deadline coming. In a week or so, the Sun sets, and something happens to the air that most people do not expect. That is the next part.

After about two weeks of daylight comes about two weeks of night. Today, the lunar surface plunges to about minus one hundred and seventy three degrees Celsius. That is the difference of three hundred degrees between noon and midnight, one of the most extreme swings of any large body in the solar system.

Now ask what the air does. Air is a heat store. Each square metre of ground sits under about six times as much air as on Earth, and that air holds a great deal of warmth. It cools slowly, because the only way for it to lose heat is to radiate it away, and thick air radiates slowly. A column of air that heavy would take months to cool off, which is far longer than the two weeks of lunar night. So the night does not get a chance to freeze the air solid.

Moving air helps too. The warm dayside air rises, and it flows across to the night side, where it sinks. This is how the planet Venus works. Venus turns once every two hundred and forty three Earth days, so its days and nights are enormously long. Yet its surface sits at about four hundred and sixty five degrees Celsius, both day and night, because its air is so thick that it carries heat all the way around. Venus has about ninety two times the surface pressure of Earth. The Moon's air would be much thinner than that, so the Moon would not be Venus. But a well known study in nineteen ninety seven, on planets that always keep one face to their star, found that even an atmosphere much thinner than ours can carry enough heat to the dark side to stop the gases from freezing out.

So the three hundred degree swing would shrink dramatically. By how much? Honestly, that is contested, and it depends on things nobody can pin down in advance. Clouds would matter, and so would how much water vapour the air picked up. The Moon has no oceans, so it would be a very dry world, with water only from ice in the dark craters at the poles. Without clouds, the sky would be clear, and the Sun would hit the ground hard.

Here is a clue about where the average would land. The Moon reflects only about eleven percent of the sunlight that hits it. Using that figure, NASA's lunar fact sheet gives a black body temperature of about two hundred and seventy kelvin, a little below the freezing point of water. That is what the Moon's surface would average with no greenhouse effect at all. Nitrogen, oxygen and argon are poor greenhouse gases. So you would expect a world hovering near the freezing mark. Not Mars cold, and not Venus hot, but something surprisingly close to a cool autumn day on Earth.

So far, the Moon with air is looking almost friendly. Almost. Now we have to talk about the bill, because the air does not stay. And how it leaves is a story with a genuine scientific argument in it.

Gas molecules are always moving, and they move at a spread of speeds. A few are always fast enough to break away. To escape the Moon from the surface, you need to move at two point three eight kilometres per second. To escape Earth, you need eleven point two. That is a gap of nearly five times. On the Moon, the high, thin top of the atmosphere, heated by sunlight, would constantly lose its fastest molecules to space. The lightest gases go first. Hydrogen and helium would be gone almost at once. Heavier molecules like nitrogen would linger, but they would leak all the same.

Then there is the Sun itself. The Moon has no global magnetic field today. It has only patches of magnetised crust. So the solar wind, a stream of charged particles flowing outward from the Sun, strikes the upper air directly, knocking molecules loose. We have a good example of how well that works. Mars has an escape velocity of about five kilometres per second, more than twice the Moon's. Yet NASA's MAVEN spacecraft found that the solar wind has stripped away a large share of the Martian atmosphere over billions of years.

The Moon does get one short break. Each month, it passes through the long tail of Earth's magnetic field, for about six days around full Moon. During that time, the solar wind is largely shielded. Whether that slows the loss in a meaningful way is something scientists do not fully agree on.

So how long would the air last? Anyone who gives you an exact number is guessing. The loss depends on the temperature of the upper air, the activity of the Sun, and details no one has measured. But there is one clue from the real Moon. Around three and a half billion years ago, volcanoes erupted across its surface and released gases. A study published in twenty seventeen by Needham and Kring estimated that those eruptions briefly gave the Moon a real atmosphere, one that may have lasted about seventy million years before it leaked away. The Moon is about four and a half billion years old. So even the lucky natural version was a short season in its life. Our imagined air would be a short season too.

Now, a twist on that story, because the Moon is not the only small world with a thick atmosphere. Saturn's moon Titan has an escape velocity of about two point six four kilometres per second, a little higher than the Moon's, and a surface gravity lower than the Moon's. Yet it has an atmosphere about one and a half times as thick as ours at the surface. The reason is temperature. Titan sits at about minus one hundred and seventy nine degrees Celsius, so its molecules move slowly, and few of them ever reach escape speed. The Moon's problem is not just that its gravity is weak. It is that it is bathed in sunlight, close to the Sun, with no shelter. Put the same Moon far out in the outer solar system, and the air might stay for ages.

But we are not done. I promised something that most people would not expect, and it comes from a place you would not look. Everything we have said about the Moon's air has been about what it takes away. Gravity is too weak, the Sun is too fierce, and the air leaks. Now, what does it give?

Think about flying. To stay in the air, a wing has to produce lift equal to your weight. On the Moon, your weight is one sixth of what it is on Earth. The air at the ground would have almost the same density as ours, about one point two kilograms per cubic metre, because the pressure and temperature are about the same. So you have air as thick as ours, and a body that weighs a sixth as much.

Here is where physics gets generous. The power you need to hold yourself up with wings does not fall in proportion to weight. It falls faster, because it scales with weight raised to the power of one and a half. Six raised to the power of one and a half is about fifteen. So the same wing, the same shape and the same size, would need about fifteen times less power to keep you aloft.

Compare that to the greatest feat of human powered flight. In nineteen seventy nine, the Gossamer Albatross, pedalled by Bryan Allen, crossed the English Channel. It flew thirty five point eight kilometres in two hours and forty nine minutes, with a pilot working at the limit of what a human body can sustain. That aircraft barely stayed up on Earth. On our air-wrapped Moon, the same machine would need only a small fraction of that effort. A person would not need a long, delicate aircraft. A pair of light wings and a few seconds of effort might be enough to glide, and a modest amount of flapping might be enough to climb. And every jump you make, which already carries you six times higher than on Earth, would turn into a soaring flight.

No one has ever tested this, so treat it as a physics estimate and not a demonstration. But the numbers point one way. The Moon, with air, would be the best place in the solar system for a human to fly. The one place where the old dream of strapping on wings and rising under your own power would work.

And that air would be rising above a world that is slowly changing. Over decades, the wind, thin as it is by Earth standards but real by lunar standards, would work on the surface. The dust would shift into ripples and drifts. That bootprint at the start? Without air, it can sit there for a very long time, because nothing disturbs it. With wind that moves grains a sixth as heavy as Earth's, it would be softened and smoothed and eventually erased. How fast depends on the weather of a world that has never had weather, so no one can say for sure. But the one place where human footprints have been safe from everything except slow dust rain, would become a place where footprints disappear.

That is the cost and the gift. The Moon would gain a blue sky, sound, sunsets that last for hours, shooting stars, a milder climate, and the ability to fly. It would lose its silence, its record of the past, and its air, slowly, to space.

So here is the question for you, and I would really like to hear your answer in the comments. If you could keep only one of these on the Moon for good, the blue sky, the sound, or the wings, which would you choose? And if the air was going to leak away in the end, would you still build it?
