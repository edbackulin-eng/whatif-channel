It is a quiet Tuesday, and you are standing on a street corner, waiting for a ride. The blue dot on your phone shows exactly where you are. Then the dot stops moving. It does not vanish. It just sits there, and a small grey circle around it begins to swell, from a few metres wide to a few hundred, then to the size of your whole neighbourhood. Above you, nothing looks different. The sky is the same blue. But every satellite in orbit has just stopped working, all of them, at the same instant.

Before we go any further, let me be plain about the premise. This will not happen. More than ten thousand active satellites circle the Earth. They belong to dozens of countries and hundreds of companies, they run on different hardware, different software and different orbits, and no single fault is known that could reach all of them at once. The closest thing nature offers is a violent solar storm, and how much damage even the worst storm could do to satellites is genuinely contested among scientists. So treat this as a thought experiment with the physics left switched on. Every satellite goes dark and stops responding. We will follow what happens next, second by second, then year by year.

And the first surprise is about the word failed. Satellites do not vanish when they die. They keep moving. Hold on to that, because it decides how this story ends.

Start with the first second. Nothing falls. Nothing explodes. At around five hundred and fifty kilometres up, a typical low orbit satellite is travelling at about seven and a half kilometres per second, and that speed does not care whether the electronics are alive. A dead satellite is just a very fast, very expensive rock, and it keeps going around the planet roughly every ninety minutes.

What stops is the signal. The first thing you would lose is the one you probably think about least. The navigation satellites of the Global Positioning System, GPS, number about thirty. They orbit at roughly twenty thousand two hundred kilometres, and each one circles the Earth twice a day. Each carries atomic clocks and broadcasts a time stamp, over and over. Your phone listens to four or more of them, compares the tiny delays, and works out where it is.

Here is the part that matters. Radio waves travel at the speed of light, and light covers about thirty centimetres in one nanosecond. So an error of one microsecond, one millionth of a second, becomes an error of about three hundred metres. That is why your position is only as good as the clocks. When the clocks go quiet, your phone does not fall back to nothing. It tries cell towers and wifi networks, and it can usually guess a rough position from them. In a city, that might be good enough to find a cafe. It will not be good enough to find a doorway.

Seconds pass. Cars with built in navigation go quiet. Ride apps cannot match you with a driver whose own dot has frozen. A delivery van stops at a junction because the screen tells it nothing. Annoying, yes. But these are the visible failures. They are not the ones that should worry you. The real damage is in places you cannot see, and it is built on something stranger than maps. I will get to it in a moment, but first, the sky itself.

Within the first minute, satellite television goes dark. Most broadcast dishes point at satellites in geostationary orbit, which sits at thirty five thousand seven hundred and eighty six kilometres above the equator. At that height a satellite takes almost exactly one rotation of the Earth to complete one lap, so it hangs over the same spot. Those satellites carry television, but also live news feeds, ship communications, and the links that connect remote airstrips and oil platforms to the world. All of that is gone. If you are on a cruise ship in the middle of an ocean, the internet you were using a second ago is now a memory.

Now the question you may be asking. Do planes fall out of the sky? No. This is worth saying clearly, because it is the most common fear. Airliners do not depend on GPS to stay in the air. They carry inertial reference systems, which use gyroscopes and accelerometers to track position from the last known point. They also use ground based radio beacons, radar, and air traffic controllers who can see them on their own radar screens. Pilots train for exactly this. Flights already in the air would land. Flights on the ground would wait. The sky would feel emptier within hours.

Now the minutes. This is where the hidden machinery begins to show.

Every mobile network in the world needs its towers to agree on the time. Not roughly. Precisely. Many cellular systems need neighbouring towers to stay within about one and a half microseconds of each other, or their signals start to collide, and calls and data degrade. Most towers get that time from GPS. When the signal vanishes, each tower switches to its own internal oscillator and begins what engineers call holdover. A good oscillator keeps decent time for a while. But no two drift at the same rate, and the drift grows. Some networks have better backup clocks than others. Some have almost none.

Financial markets rely on the same trick. European rules require firms doing high frequency trading to stamp their trades with a time accurate to within one hundred microseconds of Coordinated Universal Time. Most of them get that time from GPS. Without it, the firms have to fall back on other sources, and regulators have to decide whether trading can continue at all. Expect exchanges to halt, because a market where nobody can prove the order of events is a market where nobody can prove who traded first.

The power grid is next. Modern grids use sensors called phasor measurement units, which compare the exact phase of the electrical wave at different points hundreds of kilometres apart. They need a shared clock to do it, and the shared clock is usually GPS. Grids kept running for a century before those sensors existed, so the lights do not go out. But the operators lose some of their best tools for spotting trouble early, and they have to run the system with wider safety margins. That costs money, and in a stressed grid, it can cost reliability.

Stay with me, because there is a part of this story that most people get wrong. The loss of navigation is not the biggest problem. The loss of time is. And there is a twist in how time works that I will return to, because it turns out the world's clock has a surprising hiding place.

Now the first hour. In the first hour, the damage spreads outward from the obvious to the hidden.

Container ships and cargo vessels still have radar, compasses, charts and trained navigators, so they will not be lost at sea. But the automatic identification systems that let ships see each other and ports track them rely on GPS positions. Harbour traffic slows to a crawl. Cranes at ports rely on precise positioning to stack containers, and many of them slow down or stop. Trucks lose their routing. Delivery schedules, which were built on the assumption that everyone knows where everything is, begin to unravel.

On farms, modern tractors steer themselves along rows using GPS corrections accurate to the centimetre. That precision is what lets farmers plant, spray and harvest without wasting seed or fertiliser. Without it, the tractors still work, but a human has to drive them, slowly and less accurately. If your outage happens in planting season, that matters. A study prepared for the United States National Institute of Standards and Technology in twenty nineteen estimated that a GPS outage would cost the American economy about one billion dollars per day on average, and more during planting season. Britain commissioned a similar study, and found a loss of around one billion pounds per day. These are estimates, and they rest on assumptions, but they come from reputable analysis and they point in one direction.

Now the first day. This is where the weather begins to fail, and I want to explain why this one is so important.

Weather forecasting looks like something done by meteorologists with maps. In reality it is done by supercomputers, which take in millions of observations a day and run them through a model of the atmosphere. The European Centre for Medium Range Weather Forecasts, one of the best in the world, has said that the great majority of the observations it uses come from satellites. Polar orbiting satellites measure temperature and humidity through the whole depth of the atmosphere. Geostationary satellites such as the American GOES series watch storms form and move, every few minutes. Take them away, and the model is running on weather balloons, aircraft reports, ships, and ground stations. These still exist. But they cover far less of the world, especially the oceans.

The forecasts do not stop. They get worse. A five day forecast starts to look like a three day forecast. Hurricane tracks widen. During a busy storm season, that wider cone of uncertainty means evacuations that did not need to happen, and some that did not happen when they should have. Nobody can say precisely how much worse, because it has never been tested at this scale, and anyone who gives you a precise number is guessing.

And now a thought that may make you uneasy. Some of the satellites that just failed were never meant to be seen. Early warning satellites watch for the heat of missile launches. Reconnaissance satellites watch for troop movements. If you are a government and your eyes go dark, but you suspect your neighbours are blind too, what do you do? Analysts disagree on how dangerous this would be. Some argue that a world where everybody is blind is a cautious world. Others worry that a blind military is a nervous one. Nobody knows, and I would rather say so than pretend otherwise.

Next, the first week. This is where a quiet problem becomes a loud one, and it is a problem you have not heard about yet.

The satellites are still up there. Nobody is steering them. The ones in low orbit are always being nudged by the thin upper atmosphere, which drags on them. Operators counter that drag with small thrusters. Without thrusters, the orbits start shrinking. The satellites do not plunge, they spiral, very slowly, over months to years depending on their height.

But the more pressing issue is collisions. Today, active satellites dodge each other all the time. SpaceX reported performing well over one hundred thousand avoidance manoeuvres in a single six month period. Those manoeuvres happen because ground radar tracks objects, computers predict close approaches, and operators fire thrusters. In our scenario, the thrusters are dead, and so are the operators' links to them. The dodging stops. Meanwhile, the ground radars continue to track and warn. The warnings go nowhere.

How dangerous is that? Take the worst collision on record. In February two thousand nine, an American Iridium communications satellite collided with a defunct Russian Cosmos satellite at about seven hundred and ninety kilometres up. They closed on each other at roughly eleven point seven kilometres per second. Both were destroyed. The crash produced thousands of tracked fragments, and countless smaller ones that radar cannot see. Now imagine ten thousand satellites with no one steering them.

The good news is that space is mostly empty. Even with the dodging switched off, two particular satellites are very unlikely to meet on any given day. The risk accumulates slowly, over years. The bad news is that it accumulates, and at higher altitudes it never goes away on its own.

This brings us to an idea first described in nineteen seventy eight by two NASA scientists, Donald Kessler and Burton Cour-Palais. In what is now called the Kessler syndrome, a collision creates debris, the debris causes more collisions, and the process feeds on itself. People often imagine it as a sudden event, with a sky full of shrapnel overnight. That is not how it works. It is a slow chain reaction that plays out over decades. Whether the process has already begun in some orbital bands is debated among researchers, and I will not pretend that question is settled.

Think about how much debris there is already. The European Space Agency estimates that about a million pieces larger than one centimetre but smaller than ten are in orbit right now, and about one hundred and thirty million pieces between one millimetre and one centimetre. Most of these are too small to track. But at orbital speeds, even a tiny fragment hits with devastating force. In two thousand seven, China destroyed one of its own weather satellites in a missile test, and the resulting cloud included more than three thousand trackable pieces. In two thousand twenty one, a Russian missile test against the Cosmos 1408 satellite produced about fifteen hundred trackable fragments, and the crew of the International Space Station had to shelter in their return capsules.

So here is the question. In our scenario, what happens to those ten thousand silent satellites over the next few years? The answer depends on where they are. Hold on, because the answer is stranger than you might expect, and it contains the surprise I promised.

Let us go to the first year. The Earth does not look different from the ground, but the infrastructure looks nothing like it used to.

Governments, power companies and banks will have scrambled for alternatives. The most promising one is not a new invention. It is a very old one. Long range navigation systems based on ground transmitters, such as the modern version called eLoran, send powerful low frequency signals that are hard to jam and cannot be knocked out from space. They are not as precise as GPS, but they are good enough to provide timing and rough navigation for ships and critical infrastructure. Several countries have studied them or built them. In our scenario, there would be a rush to build more.

Time itself can also come through cables. Optical fibre networks can carry timing signals directly from national laboratories to the places that need them. This already happens in some countries, but not on a global scale. Building it out would take years.

And here, finally, is the twist I promised about time. The satellites did not create the world's time. They only broadcast it. Coordinated Universal Time is calculated by the International Bureau of Weights and Measures in France, using hundreds of atomic clocks held in dozens of national laboratories on the ground. Every one of those clocks is still ticking. Time did not stop. What stopped was the delivery service. The world's most precise clocks are sitting safely in basements, humming along, and the only thing missing is a way to carry their time to the rest of us. That is a much easier problem than building a new clock. It is a plumbing problem, not a physics one. And that, strangely, is why a full recovery is not impossible.

One more point on the internet, because you may be wondering. Roughly ninety nine percent of intercontinental data travels through undersea cables, not satellites. So the web itself mostly survives. What you lose is the web in places only satellites reach, and the timing that quietly keeps its machinery in step.

Now let us talk about the sky overhead. The geostationary belt is a special case. Satellites there sit so high that the thin atmosphere does nothing to them. Normally, operators push old satellites a few hundred kilometres higher, into a graveyard orbit, before they die. In our scenario they cannot, so the dead stay in the busiest part of the belt. Because of the pull of the Moon and the Sun, an uncontrolled satellite there slowly tilts its orbit, reaching about fifteen degrees over roughly a quarter of a century. As the tilt grows, the dead satellites start crossing the paths of each other. Collisions there happen at lower speeds than in low orbit, but they still shatter things. And the debris stays for an essentially unlimited time. That is the hard part of the story. The high orbits do not heal.

Now the surprise. Compare that with low orbit. Low orbit is a different story, and it is where most of the satellites are, with one company's constellation making up the biggest share. Remember the atmosphere nudging every satellite? In the low orbit where those satellites fly, that drag is not a nuisance. It is a broom. Starlink satellites orbit low enough that SpaceX says a dead one falls out of the sky and burns up within a few years. We even have a natural experiment. In February two thousand twenty two, a geomagnetic storm heated and swelled the upper atmosphere just after a batch of forty nine Starlink satellites was launched. The extra drag was too much. Thirty eight of them fell and burned up. And in May two thousand twenty four, the strongest geomagnetic storm in two decades, rated G5 by the United States space weather forecasters, pushed satellites around across low orbit.

So here is the turn. In our scenario, the thousands of dead satellites in low orbit do not stay. The atmosphere sweeps them away. Within a few years, most of the lowest ones would have spiralled in and burned. Crashes would happen on the way, and each one would add fragments, but the fragments are small and at that height they fall quickly too. The lowest orbits, the ones we fill most densely, clean themselves. The part that stays cluttered for centuries is the high belt, where the television satellites sit.

There is one more thing that follows from that, and almost no one talks about it. Astronomers would have a good decade. The long white streaks that bright constellations leave across telescope images would fade as the satellites came down. For the first time in years, the night sky would be darker. The loss of satellites would be a disaster for almost every human activity, and a small gift for one.

Let us go five years out. By now, the world has either rebuilt or learned to live without. And the way it rebuilds matters more than the way it fell.

First, new satellites. Launching a replacement GPS satellite takes years to build and test, and rockets would be competing for the same few launch pads. Commercial constellations can be built faster, but they also need to be certified, financed and protected. A rebuilt world would want fewer single points of failure. It would want navigation from several different systems, from ground stations, from fibre, and from clocks in every important building.

Second, a new approach to debris. Before the failure, there was an international guideline that satellites in low orbit should be removed within twenty five years of the end of their mission. After the failure, you would expect that to be enforced much faster, and for tracking and cleanup to become real industries. You would also expect arguments, because every country that launches satellites wants the freedom to do it, and no country wants to pay to clean up someone else's mess.

Third, a world that remembers. We have built a civilisation that runs on a signal most of us never think about. That signal comes from a few dozen machines, and many of the people who depend on it do not know it exists. The failure in this thought experiment is impossible, but the dependence is real. Engineers have been warning about it for years, and quietly building backups.

Now step back and look at the whole timeline. In the first seconds, your phone loses its place. In the first minutes, the invisible clocks drift. In the first hours, markets pause and ports slow down. In the first days, forecasts get worse and tractors need drivers. In the first years, the low orbits clear and the high orbits become a slow motion hazard. And the thing that held it all together, the world's time, was never really in space at all.

Which brings me to the question I would like to leave with you. If you could keep only one thing working from space, one service, one capability, which would you choose? Navigation, time, weather, communication, or the view of the Earth itself? And would your answer change if you knew the others would be gone for ten years? Tell me in the comments, and tell me what you think I got wrong.
