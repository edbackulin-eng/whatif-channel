You are standing outside on a clear night. The air is cool. Above the rooftops hangs a fat, pale Moon, and in your hand is a green laser pointer, the cheap kind that costs less than a sandwich. Around you, in every time zone where the Moon is up, billions of other people are holding the same thing. Someone counts down. On zero, every thumb presses a button. A thin green thread leaves your hand and climbs into the dark.

Here is the problem, and it is better to say it now. This cannot happen. Not everyone owns a laser pointer. Roughly half the planet is always facing away from the Moon, so those people have nothing to aim at. And no human hand is steady enough to hold a beam on a target that far away. So the premise is impossible as stated. But we are going to switch the physics on anyway and give the idea its best possible version. Call it four billion people on the Moon side of the Earth, each with a pointer, each aiming as well as a person can.

In the first few seconds, your beam is already doing something strange. And the strangest part is not what happens when it reaches the Moon. It is what happens to the beam long before it gets there. Stay with me, because the number that decides this entire video is hiding in that gap.

Start with the trip. The Moon is, on average, about three hundred eighty-four thousand kilometres away. Light covers that in about one point three seconds. So you press the button, and for one and a third seconds nothing at all happens anywhere near the Moon. The light is simply in transit, crossing a vacuum that holds nothing to scatter it.

The first problem is the one you can feel in your wrist. A laser pointer does not make a perfect pencil of light. Its beam spreads, and a typical pointer spreads by about a milliradian, which means one metre of width for every kilometre of distance. Do the multiplication. Over the distance to the Moon, that is a spot roughly three hundred eighty-four kilometres across.

That sounds enormous, and it is. But the Moon is about three thousand four hundred seventy-four kilometres wide. Your spot would cover about one ninth of its diameter. And the Moon in your sky is only about half a degree wide, which is less than the width of your little fingernail at arm's length. A hand holding a pointer wobbles by more than that. So even on a good day, your green dot is not sitting still on the Moon. It is wandering across the surface, off the edge, and into empty space beyond.

So on the very first count, a large share of the light never touches the Moon at all. Each beam slides across the lunar disc and off the side, flickering on and off the target as hands tremble. If you want a feel for the scale of the problem, imagine trying to hit a dinner plate across a football stadium with a garden hose held by someone who has just run up a flight of stairs.

Now to the number I promised. Suppose, generously, that all four billion beams land on the same patch of the Moon. How much light is that? A legal laser pointer in most countries is limited to five milliwatts. That is the standard cap for the common visible pointer, and it is the upper end of the class that regulators treat as relatively safe for brief exposure. Four billion pointers at five milliwatts each makes twenty million watts. Twenty megawatts. That is about what a few hundred electric kettles would draw, all gathered into one beam.

Twenty megawatts feels like a lot. It is not. And in the next minute, I will show you what it is up against.

At about two point five six seconds after the button press, something happens that you will never see. If your beam is lucky, a sliver of it lands on the Moon, and that sliver returns, because light that hits the Moon does not all vanish. The Moon is surprisingly dark. Its surface reflects only about twelve percent of the light that strikes it, which makes it closer to worn asphalt than to the silver coin you see in the sky. The rest of the light is absorbed and turns into the tiniest amount of heat.

So the two-second mark is quiet. The beams arrive, the Moon swallows almost all of them, and nothing flashes. No glow, no spark, no point of light that anyone on Earth could see with the naked eye. Hold that thought, because I want to show you why not, with a comparison that puts your whole planet's effort in its place.

Sunlight falls on the Moon at about thirteen hundred sixty watts for every square metre. That figure is called the solar constant, and it has been measured by satellites for decades. Now spread your twenty megawatts over that spot of about one hundred fifteen thousand square kilometres. Each square metre gets about one ten-thousandth of a watt. Compare that with the thirteen hundred sixty from the Sun. All of humanity's lasers add up to about one eight-millionth of what the Sun is already delivering to that same patch of ground.

Think about what that means. If you could stand on the Moon at that spot, in lunar daylight, you could not tell whether the lasers were on or off. Eight million people's worth of pointers would add up to the glow of one pointer in sunshine. And that is the best case, before we count the light lost on the way.

Because the way is not empty at first. The first hundred kilometres of the trip is the Earth's atmosphere, and your beam has to cross it. Air scatters green light. Dust and haze scatter it more. Anyone who has seen a laser beam in a foggy yard knows how much of the light leaks sideways. On a clear night, much of the beam gets through. On a hazy one, much less. Either way, whatever you lose is gone before it ever sees the Moon.

So by the one-minute mark, you have a scene that looks nothing like the movies. Nothing is glowing. Nothing is burning. Four billion people are holding up their arms for no visible reward. But hold on, because the minutes ahead contain a real danger, and it has nothing to do with the Moon.

Here is the part of this story that actually matters on Earth. Your beam does not stop being dangerous just because the target is far away. The first few hundred metres in front of you is where a pointer is hazardous, and the sky above you is not empty. It is full of aircraft.

Pointing a laser at a plane is already a real problem. In 2023 the United States Federal Aviation Administration recorded more than thirteen thousand laser incidents involving aircraft, a record for that country. That is what happens when a small fraction of the public does something careless. Now imagine four billion people all pointing upward at the same moment, in the same direction, toward the same patch of sky.

Pilots flying anywhere near a city would find the night lit by thousands of green threads. A laser flash in a cockpit does not need to burn anything to cause harm. It dazzles. It leaves a bright afterimage and can make it hard to read instruments, particularly during landing or takeoff, when a pilot's attention is most needed. Most of the harm would come from distraction and temporary blindness rather than lasting damage, but nobody would want to test that in the dark with a hundred passengers behind them.

Satellites and telescopes would suffer too. Astronomers rely on dark skies and sensitive detectors, and scattered green light across the atmosphere would wash out a night of observing. Some observatories would simply close for the night. So the first real casualty of this thought experiment is not the Moon. It is the sky.

And I want you to notice how the story keeps turning against our expectations. We started with a dramatic idea, a planet shooting at a rock, and the first real consequences are an aviation safety problem and a bad night for astronomers. But the Moon, in a deep sense, still has not been heard from. There is one more kind of consequence, and it is the smallest one imaginable. It is also the one that physics says is certain. Keep watching, because that one comes with a twist about atoms.

Move forward to the hour mark. The Earth turns at fifteen degrees every hour, and that turns out to wreck the whole plan. The Moon, which seemed fixed in the sky, is actually sliding steadily toward the horizon. A few hours in, your Moon is low, and the beam is passing through more and more air. Low in the sky, your light has to travel through a much thicker slab of atmosphere before it escapes. It scatters more, and fewer of the photons make it out.

Then the Moon sets. For a person standing in your spot, the beam simply has nothing to aim at. Within about half a day, everyone who started this experiment has lost their target, and the lasers have to be switched off or turned toward the dark. Meanwhile a fresh set of people, on the other side of the Earth, finds the Moon rising in their sky. They pick up the baton. The experiment is a relay race across the planet, with the baton handed on every few hours.

That is the first big surprise of the timeline. For this idea to work for a day, it takes more than four billion people. It takes the right four billion at each hour, and the group has to change continuously as the Earth turns. Now the premise is not just impossible. It is a logistics problem across eight billion people and twenty-four time zones.

Then there is the problem of seeing the target at all. The Moon is not always a bright disc. At new Moon, it sits close to the Sun in the sky and is up in the daytime, invisible against the glare. You cannot aim at what you cannot see. So on the new Moon, the lasers have no target, and you would have to point by guess at an empty patch of blue.

That timing matters for the next stretch. Over the days that follow, the Moon rises about fifty minutes later each night, because it is moving along its orbit while the Earth turns. The full cycle of phases takes about twenty-nine and a half days. So after the first day, the people holding the lasers keep finding the Moon in a slightly different place at a slightly different hour. The experiment needs endless coordination to keep going.

Here is a thought to hold on to as we go on. We have established that most of the light does not arrive, that what arrives is dwarfed by the Sun, and that the experiment cannot even run continuously. So does the Moon notice at all? There is a real answer, and it comes from a simple fact. Light carries momentum. When it hits something, it pushes. Let's compute how hard.

Light pushes with a force equal to its power divided by the speed of light. Take your twenty megawatts, and divide by three hundred million metres per second. You get about seven hundredths of a newton. That is the weight of a small coin, or roughly seven grams on a kitchen scale. That is the full push of humanity's lasers, if every one hit the Moon and every photon was absorbed.

Now compare that with what it has to move. The Moon weighs about seventy-three sextillion kilograms. That is seven point three five followed by twenty-two zeros. Divide the tiny force by that enormous mass, and you get an acceleration so small that it has almost no meaning in everyday language.

Let the lasers shine on the Moon for a whole year, without a break. Not even the most dedicated planet could manage that, but allow it. After twelve months, the Moon will have shifted by less than a billionth of a metre. About half a nanometre. That is roughly the width of a couple of atoms.

Pause on that, because it is the heart of the answer. Eight billion people, a year of effort, the full power of every pointer on a continent-sized scale, and the result is that the Moon moves by the width of a few atoms. Moving a mountain with a feather does not capture it. The mountain would have to be the size of a planet, and the feather would have to be a single stroke across a whole year.

Now compare that with something the Moon does on its own, with no help from anyone. The Moon is drifting away from the Earth at about three point eight centimetres every year. That is roughly the speed that your fingernails grow. It is not caused by light. It is caused by tides. The Moon's gravity raises bulges in the ocean, the spinning Earth drags them slightly ahead, and that bulge pulls the Moon forward in its orbit, lifting it into a wider path.

So in the same year that humanity's lasers shift the Moon by a few atoms, the tides carry it away by nearly four centimetres. The tides win by a factor of roughly a hundred million. The Moon never notices.

And that brings me to the twist, the one most people do not see coming. The idea of pointing lasers at the Moon sounds like a fantasy or a threat. But humans have been doing exactly that for more than fifty years. And the reason is the whole reason we know that three point eight centimetre number.

In July of 1969, the Apollo 11 astronauts placed a panel on the lunar surface. It was a small array of mirrors, one hundred of them, each a corner cube, a shape that reflects light straight back to where it came from, no matter the angle. Later missions left more. Apollo 14 added another array of one hundred. Apollo 15 left one with three hundred. The Soviet Union also landed reflectors on its robotic rovers.

Ever since, scientists have fired laser pulses at those mirrors and timed the round trip. The method is called lunar laser ranging. It works. Observatories on Earth do exactly what our imaginary crowd would do, only with far better aim and far more patience. They point a laser at a spot on the Moon no larger than a mirror panel, and they count the photons that come back.

And here is the astonishing part. The returns are so feeble that the best stations catch only a few photons at a time, from a pulse made of unimaginably many. But from timing those few, researchers can measure the distance between the Earth and the Moon to about a millimetre. A millimetre, across nearly four hundred thousand kilometres. That is how the three point eight centimetres a year was nailed down in the first place.

So the real answer to our question is a little ironic. Pointing lasers at the Moon is not a stunt. It is one of the most productive experiments in the history of planetary science. And a single well-aimed beam from a single observatory has taught us more than any crowd of pointers ever could.

What has it taught us? Here is where the story takes its sharpest turn. By watching how the Moon wobbles as it orbits, and measuring that wobble to such fine detail, researchers found evidence that the Moon has a fluid core. Not a solid ball all the way through, but a liquid layer deep inside, still molten after four and a half billion years. A signal about the Moon's heart, read from the light bouncing off mirrors on its surface.

Those same measurements have also tested one of the deepest ideas in physics. Einstein's theory says that the Earth and the Moon should fall toward the Sun at exactly the same rate, even though the Earth is made of denser material and has a heavier core. Laser ranging has checked that claim to within roughly a part in ten trillion. And Einstein's idea has held up. If the Moon had fallen even slightly differently from the Earth, the distance to it would have wobbled in a particular pattern, and the mirrors would have caught it.

Now for the part where I tell you where the science is not settled. That three point eight centimetres per year is what we see today. But if you run the clock backwards at that steady rate, the Moon would have been touching the Earth far too recently, well before its estimated age of four and a half billion years. The numbers do not fit. So the rate must have changed over time. How it changed, and why, is still debated. It depends on the ancient oceans, on how the continents were arranged, and on how much the tides could flex. Researchers disagree about the history, even though they agree about the present.

So at the end of our timeline, what is the answer? On the scale of seconds, the beams arrive after one point three seconds, and almost nothing is seen. On the scale of minutes, the beams wander off the Moon, and the real danger is to pilots and observers on the ground. Over hours, the Moon sets, the Earth turns, and the experiment passes from one side of the planet to the other. Across days, it keeps losing the Moon at new Moon and finding it again an hour later each night. And over years, the push on the Moon amounts to the width of a few atoms, while the tides carry it away by centimetres.

The deepest answer is a reversal of the one we expected. We began by asking what we could do to the Moon. We ended up learning what the Moon, quietly, can tell us. A pointer in your hand can do almost nothing to a world. But light sent up and caught on its way back can measure that world to the width of a pencil line, and can reach through its crust to its core.

There is one more thing worth sitting with. That faint green beam in your hand is made of about thirteen quadrillion photons every second. Each one is a tiny packet of energy, and each one carries a little push. Not one of them is wasted in principle. Many scatter into the air. Many are absorbed by the dust of the lunar soil. And a very few, if you were incredibly lucky and had a mirror waiting for them, would come all the way home.
