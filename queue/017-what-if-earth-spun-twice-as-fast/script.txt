Stand on the equator at dawn and look east. The ground under your feet is moving that way at about 1,670 kilometres per hour. That is faster than sound travels through the air around you, which is about 1,225 kilometres per hour at sea level. You feel nothing, because the air, the ocean and you are all carried along together. Now make the planet turn twice as fast. Same sun, same sky. But the sun climbs and sets in about six hours, and the night is over in six more. Your patch of ground is now racing along at about 3,340 kilometres per hour, more than two and a half times the speed of sound. The first thing that goes wrong is not the wind. It is something much quieter, and it takes thousands of years to finish.

Before we go any further, a plain fact. This cannot happen. Nothing in the solar system can double the spin of the Earth. The planet's rotation stores roughly two hundred thousand trillion trillion joules. Double the spin and that figure quadruples, so you would need to add three times as much again. That is about half an hour of the entire output of the Sun, every photon it sends in every direction, poured into one planet's spin. And energy is only half the problem. Spin is also angular momentum, which cannot be created from nothing, and there is nothing out there to push. So this is a thought experiment, and we will run it under one generous rule. At the instant it begins, the rock, the oceans, the air and you are all already moving at the new speed. Nobody is flung anywhere. After that, the physics stays switched on.

That rule spares us the Hollywood disaster. It does not spare us the real one, and the real one starts with your own weight.

Seconds. Anything moving in a circle needs a force pulling it toward the centre of that circle. On a spinning planet, a small part of gravity is spent doing exactly that job, which means a small part of gravity is not available to hold you down. At the equator today, this costs you about one part in 289 of your weight. That is a little over a third of one percent. The cost grows with the square of the spin rate. Double the spin and it quadruples, to about one part in 72, which is a bit under one and a half percent. So at the equator you would weigh roughly one percent less than you do now. At the poles, nothing changes, because a point on the axis just turns on the spot. Today the equator already measures 9.78 metres per second squared, against 9.83 at the poles. Part of that is this effect, and part of it is that the planet is already fatter at the equator. A one percent change in weight sounds like nothing. The oceans disagree.

Minutes. Earth is not a sphere. Its radius at the equator is about 6,378 kilometres, and at the poles about 6,357. That puts the equator roughly 21 kilometres further from the centre. The spin did that. Over geological time the planet slumped into the shape that a spinning ball of rock and water settles into. Now the outward push has quadrupled, and the comfortable shape changes with it. To a first approximation, the bulge scales with the square of the spin, so the new comfortable shape has an equatorial bulge about four times bigger. That is on the order of eighty kilometres. The rock cannot follow that quickly. Stone flows, but it flows slowly. The ocean has no such problem. Water tries to take the new shape at once, so the sea surface begins to tilt, away from the poles and toward the equator, relative to the land. The tilt is a fraction of a degree. But it is spread across thousands of kilometres, and a small slope over a huge distance adds up to a lot of height.

How much? A crude calculation that keeps the old sea floor fixed puts the new sea surface kilometres higher than today at the equator, and kilometres lower than today near the poles. Kilometres. The average ocean is only about 3,700 metres deep. So in this simple picture the polar seas do not just fall, they run dry, and the tropics drown. Hold on to that, because it is the clean answer, and the real answer is messier. The mess is the interesting part.

Hours. The mess is caused by the Coriolis effect. Water that starts moving toward the equator does not keep going straight, because the planet turns underneath it. In the northern hemisphere moving water is bent to the right, in the southern hemisphere to the left, and with the spin doubled, the sideways push doubles with it. Within a few hours the moving water has been turned until it runs along the slope instead of down it. Oceanographers call this geostrophic balance. It is the reason real currents, like the Gulf Stream, can run along a tilted sea surface without ever falling down it. So the sea does not simply slosh to the equator and settle. It locks into vast new currents that hold the tilt in place. Only friction, with the sea floor and with the wind, slowly wears those currents down, and the water creeps equatorward as they fade. I would guess that takes months to years rather than hours, but that is an informed guess, not a measured fact. Nobody has run this exact sudden change in a full ocean model that I can point you to. So treat the ending as firm and the route as a sketch.

And the ending is a different map. Water gathers in a deep belt around the tropics. Coastal cities there, which sit a few metres above today's sea level, are gone. Meanwhile the old sea floor at high latitudes stands exposed. In this crude picture the Bering Strait is dry, and so are the shallow shelves of the Arctic, so Asia and North America are joined again, as they were during the last ice age. Great stretches of old seabed become new continents, made of mud and salt, with no rivers and no towns. Ports in the far north and south find themselves a long way from any water. Before the thousands of years are up, the rock has its say as well. But first, look up, because the sky has been rewritten, and it is about to change how you live.

Days. Measured against the stars, the day is now about twelve hours long. Today it is 23 hours, 56 minutes and 4 seconds. The year does not care. Earth's orbit around the Sun is untouched, so a year is still about 365 and a quarter of today's days. That works out to roughly 731 of the new, short days. The tilt of the axis stays at about 23.4 degrees, so the seasons arrive on the old schedule. Only the days between them are cut in half.

The tides change too. The Moon still raises its two bulges in the ocean, and the strength of that pull has not changed. But the planet now turns under the Moon about every twelve hours rather than every twenty four and a half. At the coast, high tide would arrive roughly every six hours instead of every twelve and a bit. Whether that makes tides bigger or smaller depends on the shapes of the ocean basins, and the basins have just been rearranged, so nobody can say.

Your body is the next problem. The human body clock runs a little slow, at about 24 and a fifth hours, according to careful laboratory work by Charles Czeisler and colleagues. When volunteers were put on artificial days far from 24 hours, such as 28 hours, their body clocks did not follow. They kept ticking at their own pace. A twelve-hour day would be hopeless. You would live by the wall clock and the lamp, not by the sun, and your biology would shrug. Six hours of darkness is also not enough for a night, so the idea of a single long sleep would give way to something stranger. People would find some arrangement. Whether it would be healthy is a very different question.

Plants have the same problem, and they cannot choose to ignore it. They keep their own clocks of roughly 24 hours, and a 2005 experiment by Antony Dodd and colleagues, published in the journal Science, showed that the small plant Arabidopsis grew better and survived better when the period of its internal clock matched the cycle of light and dark around it. Put a crop on a day half as long as its clock expects, and that advantage is gone. Plants can be bred, so farming would recover, but the early harvests would very likely suffer.

The weather has a bigger problem. The Coriolis effect is what spins a tropical cyclone, and it is the reason storms of that kind do not form within about five degrees of the equator. Double the spin and the force doubles, so the same threshold is reached at half the distance, about two and a half degrees. The dead zone shrinks. Whether the storms get stronger, I can't promise you. That is not settled.

What is better understood is the big circulation of the air. Today, warm air rises at the equator, travels toward about 30 degrees of latitude, and sinks, which is why the world's great deserts sit in those bands. Climate model experiments that change the rotation rate, such as the one by Navarra and Boccaletti in 2002, and a later survey of rapidly rotating planets by Kaspi and Showman, find the same pattern. The faster the spin, the narrower the tropical overturning cell, and the more numerous the jet streams and weather belts. The deserts would move toward the equator, and the world would be striped with more, thinner bands of wind and rain. How far they move, the models disagree. The days themselves also cool less, because the nights last only six hours. The daily swing in temperature shrinks, and the bitter desert nights of today would soften.

There is one winner. Rockets launched eastward from the equator today borrow about 465 metres per second from the planet's spin. Low orbit needs about 7.8 kilometres per second, so that gift is about six percent of the job. Double the spin and the gift doubles, to about 930 metres per second, nearly twelve percent. Every launch gets cheaper. If that is the only good news, it is very good news for a very small number of people.

Weeks and months. Back to the ground, because that is where the real violence is. The planet wants to change its shape by tens of kilometres, and the crust under your feet is not a liquid. It is a shell of rock, already cracked into plates, and it will not stretch quietly. The equator wants to bulge out. The poles want to be drawn in. Every fault line on the planet becomes a place where that stress can let go. The largest earthquake ever recorded, in Chile in 1960, reached magnitude 9.5, according to the United States Geological Survey. A planet being reshaped would need to deliver a great many events that large, or an unbroken stream of smaller ones. How much of it comes as earthquakes and how much as slow creep is not known. All we can say is that nobody lives through this comfortably.

Years. Now the rock begins to move. The mantle behaves like an extremely stiff fluid, and we can measure how stiff, because it is still responding to the last ice age. Parts of Scandinavia and Canada are rising today, thousands of years after their ice sheets melted. That is the timescale for a planet to change shape. Over thousands of years, the equatorial rock rises and the polar rock sinks, and the sea, which was piled against the tropics, spreads back out to meet the new level. The map slowly relaxes into a planet with an equator nearly four times as swollen as now.

There is one more part of the planet we need to ask about, deep inside. The Earth's magnetic field is made in the liquid outer core, and that liquid is stirred by the planet's rotation. You might expect a faster spin to mean a stronger field. A well-known scaling study by Christensen and Aubert in 2006 found that, for fast-rotating dynamos like ours, the field strength depends on how much heat is flowing out of the core, not directly on the spin rate. If that is right, the field barely notices. But the study is a model, and the core is not something we can see, so this is a place where researchers differ about how far the result can be pushed.

Centuries and beyond. Now the Moon takes over, because the Moon is the only brake the Earth has. The ocean tides drag on the sea floor, and that friction slows the Earth by about two milliseconds per century, according to historical records of eclipses. The angular momentum does not vanish. It goes into the Moon's orbit, which is why the Moon is moving away from us by about 3.8 centimetres every year, a number measured by bouncing lasers off mirrors left by the Apollo astronauts. On a twelve-hour day, the same friction would be working on a faster-spinning planet, and the Moon would be nudged outward faster. How much faster is uncertain, because tidal friction depends on the shapes of the ocean basins, and in this world they are completely different. For scale, if the braking stayed at today's pace, putting the twelve hours back would take more than two billion years.

Here is the part that makes this less alien than it sounds. Earth has spun this fast before. Fossil corals from about 400 million years ago record daily growth lines, and the lines per year add up to roughly 400 days, which means a day of about 22 hours. That is the evidence behind a famous paper by John Wells in 1963. And much earlier, right after the giant impact that made the Moon, some models give the young Earth a day of only a few hours. Just how short is debated, and competing versions of that story exist, from Robin Canup and from Matija Cuk and Sarah Stewart. So a fast Earth is not a fantasy. It is a page from our own planet's early history, with the continents in the wrong places.

Now the twist I promised, and it is in orbit. Satellites obey a simple rule. The longer the orbit, the higher the altitude. Today, a satellite takes exactly one turn of the Earth, about 24 hours, when it flies at 35,786 kilometres above the equator. That is the geostationary orbit. From the ground it hangs motionless, which is why so many television and weather satellites live there. Now halve the length of the day. A satellite needs to complete its orbit in half the time, and the rule says the altitude drops, not by half, but by a smaller amount, to about 20,200 kilometres. And that number should ring a bell. It is the altitude of the GPS satellites. They were built to circle the Earth twice in each sidereal day, an orbit of about 11 hours and 58 minutes. On the faster Earth, that is exactly one orbit per day. The orbit that carries your navigation today is the new parking orbit.

The old parking orbit is the casualty. Every satellite at 35,786 kilometres is now too slow for the new day, so from the ground it drifts westward across the sky at about 15 degrees every hour. A dish pointed at a television satellite loses it within minutes. The whole belt that carries so much of the world's broadcasting and weather watching would need to be replaced. And the GPS satellites would not hang still either, because their orbits are tilted about 55 degrees to the equator, so each would trace a slow figure eight above one patch of ground, day after day, and arrive back where it started. It is not stillness, but it is a pattern, and a pattern is something engineers can build on.

So what you end up with is a planet that did not break, did not fly apart, and did not hurl you into space. For that, the equator would have to carry you around the planet in about 84 minutes, which is a spin about seventeen times faster than today. Twice as fast is a long way short of that. What you get instead is a planet that is quietly, permanently remade. A sea heaped against the tropics. A sky of narrow bands. A six-hour night. And an old network of satellites suddenly flying at the wrong height for the world beneath them.

Which brings me to a question for you. You get to choose where to live on this world. You can take the drowned, crowded, warm tropical shore with its new deep sea, or the high, dry, cold floor of the old polar ocean, newly exposed, with a long way to the water. Where would you settle, and what would you do about sleep? Tell me in the comments.
