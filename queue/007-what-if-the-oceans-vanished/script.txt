===NARRATION===
You are standing on a beach at dawn. A wave is about to reach your ankles. Then it does not arrive, because the wave is gone. The whole ocean is gone, everywhere on Earth, in a single instant. The sand under your feet is still wet, and it tilts away from you, past the rocks and the pier, down toward a horizon that has dropped out of sight. Far below, something huge and silver is lying on the mud. In a few minutes you will understand why that is the least strange thing about this morning. And by the end of this video you will see why even the Moon would notice what just happened.

Let us be plain about the rules. This cannot happen. The oceans hold about 1.3 billion cubic kilometres of water, which is 96.5 percent of all the water on Earth. No known process can take that away, and nothing in physics lets matter simply blink out of existence. So we are cheating, on purpose. We delete the water and nothing else. The salt stays. The mud stays. Every fish, whale and strand of kelp stays, stranded. Then we switch the physics back on and watch what it does. We will go from seconds to minutes, then hours, days, years, and finally thousands of years. And the part that should worry you most arrives within minutes, and it comes from above.

Start with the seconds. The first thing you notice is the silence. No surf, no gulls working the waterline, no hiss of foam. Just a very large, very quiet slope. You are standing at the top of a long staircase, because the oceans cover 71 percent of the planet and the ground beneath them is anything but flat.

First comes the continental shelf, a gentle apron of land running out from every coast. Its outer edge lies at a depth of around 130 metres. You could walk that far in an hour or two, across sand, shells and the wrecks of old ships. Then the shelf ends, and the ground falls away. The average depth of the ocean is about 3.7 kilometres. The deepest point of the Grand Canyon is about 1.8 kilometres. So the average drop from the edge of the shelf to the plains below is roughly twice as deep as the Grand Canyon, and in places it is far more.

Out on those plains stand mountains that nobody has ever seen dry. Mauna Kea, measured from its base on the seafloor, rises more than ten kilometres, taller than Everest at 8,849 metres. Winding between the plains is the mid-ocean ridge, a chain of volcanic mountains about 65,000 kilometres long, the longest mountain range on the planet. And at the bottom of the Mariana Trench, the floor lies nearly 11 kilometres below the old sea surface. You could drop Everest into that trench, and its summit would still sit over two kilometres below where the waves used to be.

Here is a small oddity to hold on to. Every altitude you have ever read on a map is measured from sea level. With no sea, that number needs a new definition, and surveyors would fall back on the shape of Earth's gravity, which is where sea level was always quietly pointing. The map survives. The scenery is not the danger, though. The danger is the air above you, which has just lost its floor.

Now the first minutes. Here is something nobody warns you about. Air has weight. At the sea surface the whole atmosphere presses down on you with a force you never feel, because you were born inside it. That pressure drops as you climb and rises as you descend. Now the ocean basins are empty holes, and the atmosphere does what any fluid does. It pours in. Air that hung over the water sinks into the new low ground, and the air already down there is squeezed by the weight above it. There is only so much air to go around, and suddenly there is far more low ground for it to fill.

So the pressure at your old beach falls. My own back of the envelope estimate is that it drops by roughly a quarter, to about what you would find on a mountain two and a half kilometres high. Treat that as a sketch, not a result. Nobody has run this scenario in a proper climate model. But the direction is certain. You are standing at sea level, and you feel as if you have just climbed a mountain. Your ears pop. Each breath carries less oxygen. Meanwhile, out on the abyssal plains, the pressure is higher than anything sea level has ever offered you, and the air is warmer, because air heats up when it is compressed on its way down. Thin and cool at the coast, thick and warm in the basins, and a gradient between them that the atmosphere will spend days trying to even out, with winds nobody alive has ever felt.

And the wind is not even the main event. Something inside that air is on a countdown, and it is shorter than you think.

Look up. There are clouds, and there is humid air, and for the first few hours the sky looks exactly as it did yesterday. But it is a bank account with no income. All the water vapour in the atmosphere, added together, comes to only about 12,900 cubic kilometres, a speck next to the ocean that just vanished. And a molecule of water does not stay in the air for long. On average it comes back down as rain or snow after about nine days. Normally the sea refills the sky without a pause, because about 86 percent of all evaporation on Earth comes off the ocean. That supply is now zero.

So the first rains fall, and the next, and the clouds are not replaced. Rivers keep running for a while, because rain that already fell is still travelling downhill. But they run toward basins that have become cliffs and canyons, and nothing is coming behind them. Hold on to that picture of a river with no sea. The next part is about what the sky does when it empties, and it is the part that turns a strange morning into an extinction.

By the second week, the sky is dry. Now the real change begins, because water vapour is not only rain waiting to happen. It is a blanket. A NASA study of the greenhouse effect found that water vapour and clouds together account for about three quarters of it, with carbon dioxide supplying most of the rest. Pull the vapour out and the planet loses most of that blanket. For scale, an Earth with no greenhouse effect at all would average about minus 18 degrees Celsius. We would not fall that far, because carbon dioxide stays behind, and some gas that was dissolved in the sea would escape into the air.

And here the science is genuinely unsettled. Clouds also reflect sunlight, so losing them lets more sun reach the ground, which offsets part of the cooling. Most researchers expect the loss of vapour to dominate, but nobody can give you a trustworthy global average for this world. Anyone who quotes one is guessing.

What we can say is that the swings would grow. Today the ocean smooths the planet out. The top few metres of the sea hold as much heat as the entire atmosphere above it, and the sea gives that heat back slowly through every night and every winter. Without it, and without humid air to hold the warmth, the nights fall hard. Look at the Moon, which has no atmosphere at all. Its surface reaches about 127 degrees Celsius in daylight and falls to about minus 173 at night. Earth would never go that far, because it keeps its thick air. But the extremes we already know point in the direction of travel. The hottest air temperature officially recognised on Earth is 56.7 degrees Celsius, in Death Valley. The coldest reliably measured is minus 89.2, at Vostok station in Antarctica. Both places are dry, and in this world dry is everywhere. Expect swings between noon and midnight that no coastal city has ever seen.

Now let us talk about the people standing in that world, because the next few paragraphs are not kind.

The United Nations counts more than eight billion of us. About 40 percent live within 100 kilometres of a coast. On that first morning their harbours are cliff top car parks. Ships in port tip over into the mud. Ships out at sea fall for kilometres before they hit the ground. The sea carries about 80 percent of world trade by volume, and all of that stops, in a single moment. Aquatic foods supply about 17 percent of the animal protein people eat. That is gone too.

But the ocean is also the reason rain falls on farms, and that is the bigger loss. Crops need rain, and the rain has ended. Nobody can honestly count survivors. People near deep aquifers, big lakes or mountain glaciers might hold on longer. Glaciers and ice caps hold about 1.7 percent of all Earth's water, and in this world that makes them nearly the only water left on the surface. But ice in dry air does not simply melt. It slowly sublimates, and dry cold air is thirsty. A few places will have water for a while. Most will not.

We are not the main victims, though. Most life on Earth lives where we have almost no picture of it, and the numbers are not what you would guess.

In 2018 three researchers, Yinon Bar-On, Rob Phillips and Ron Milo, added up the living mass on Earth. They put it at around 550 gigatonnes of carbon. Plants account for roughly 450 of those. All the life in the ocean, every fish, whale, jellyfish and drop of plankton, comes to only about 6 gigatonnes. That is about one percent. The sea is the biggest habitat on the planet by volume, yet it holds a thin slice of the living mass, because most of its residents are tiny, quick and short lived, while a forest stands for centuries.

So the stranded sea life is not a huge pile compared with the land. But it rots, and the smell is the least of it. You may have heard that the oceans make most of our oxygen, and you may be waiting for the part where everyone suffocates. It does not happen. NOAA says at least half of the oxygen in the air is produced by the ocean, mostly by plankton. You will see bigger figures online, and the answer depends on how you count. And most of what plankton produce is used up again by the ocean's own animals and microbes. Meanwhile the atmosphere is about 21 percent oxygen, an enormous reserve built up over billions of years. Even if all the plankton die and the rot eats some of it, the air stays breathable for a very long time. Nobody suffocates in this world. They die of thirst, cold and hunger.

The oxygen survives. But the ocean does leave something behind, and it is bigger than you think.

After a year, the world has settled into a new shape. The old seabed is a plain of mud, rock and drying brine. All that salt was dissolved in the water, and now it has nowhere to be. Spread across every continent, the salt of the ocean would form a layer about 150 metres thick, taller than most office towers. On the seafloor it would be thinner, but it would also gather in the deepest basins as the last pools dry, and the winds would pick up the dust and carry it across the continents.

Earth has done a small version of this before. Between about 5.97 and 5.33 million years ago, the Mediterranean Sea was cut off from the Atlantic. It evaporated, at least in part, and left behind a layer of salt up to about two kilometres thick, now buried under the modern seabed. Geologists still argue about how dry it got. Some picture a deep desert basin. Others picture a chain of salty lakes. Whatever it was, the ending was violent. A study published in Nature in 2009 estimated that when the Atlantic broke back through at Gibraltar, water poured in at a peak of about 100 million cubic metres per second, hundreds of times the flow of the Amazon, and the basin refilled in somewhere between a few months and two years. So this planet has seen dry sea floors, and it has seen what comes after. The difference here is that nothing is coming back. There is no Atlantic to break through, because there is no Atlantic.

Everything so far has happened on the surface. The planet underneath is about to start moving, and it moves in a way we have already watched happen once.

Rock behaves like an extremely thick liquid over very long times. When the great ice sheets of the last ice age melted, the land beneath them began to rise, and it is still rising. Around Hudson Bay in Canada, GPS stations show the ground lifting by about one centimetre a year, long after the ice has gone. That is the mantle slowly flowing back in underneath. Now think about what has just been lifted off the seafloor. An average of 3.7 kilometres of water, which is about a third as dense as the rock below it. A simple buoyancy estimate says the floor should rise by roughly a kilometre, over thousands of years. The great staircase of this world slowly becomes less steep, and nobody alive will see it finish.

Beneath that sits a bigger question. Water is not only on the surface of this planet. Seawater seeps into the crust at the mid-ocean ridges, and some of it rides down into the mantle at subduction zones, where one plate dives beneath another. Many geologists think this water helps rock deform and slip, and that it is part of why Earth has plate tectonics at all. Plates today move a few centimetres a year, about as fast as your fingernails grow. Without an ocean, no fresh water goes down. Does the machine slow, or stall? That is genuinely contested. Some models say it winds down over millions of years. Others say the weight of the sinking slabs keeps pulling the plates along regardless. Nobody knows, because we have only one planet to test it on.

And there may be a twist hiding in the rock. Tiny pieces of a mineral called ringwoodite, trapped inside a diamond from Brazil and reported in Nature in 2014, contained water locked in their crystal structure. That hints that part of the deep mantle is wet. How wet is debated. Some estimates suggest the mantle could hold as much water as the oceans, or more. Others say far less. If it is a lot, then the surface ocean was never the whole story, and the planet we just deleted the water from is thirstier inside than we thought.

But none of that is the thing I promised you at the start. That one involves the Moon.

The Moon is leaving us. Mirrors left on its surface by the Apollo astronauts let scientists bounce lasers off them, and the result is that the Moon drifts away from Earth by about 3.8 centimetres every year. The reason is the ocean. The Moon's gravity drags the seas into a bulge. Because Earth spins faster than the Moon orbits, friction carries that bulge slightly ahead of the Moon, and its gravity tugs the Moon forward and outward. The price is paid by Earth's spin. Our days are getting longer, by roughly two milliseconds every century. Most of this happens in the shallow seas, where tidal energy is turned into heat. A study in Nature in 2000 put the total at around 3.7 terawatts.

Now delete the ocean. There is no water left to slosh and rub against the seabed. The solid Earth still flexes with the tides, by tens of centimetres, but rock is far stiffer than water, and it dissipates only a small fraction of that energy. The Moon's retreat would slow sharply. The lengthening of the day would nearly stop. I want to be careful here. Estimates of how much the solid Earth contributes differ, and the rebounding crust nudges the rotation as well, so nobody has a clean number. But the principle is solid. The ocean is the brake. Take away the brake, and the slow clock that has been stretching your days for billions of years nearly stops.

So the vanishing of the oceans would not only change the weather and the map. It would change the dance between the Earth and the Moon.

There is a slow, natural version of this story, and it is real. The Sun brightens over time, and in roughly a billion years or more, the oceans are expected to be lost to space. The timing is debated, and models disagree by hundreds of millions of years. But even then the result is not our scenario. In ours, the air stays thick. Mars, by comparison, has an atmosphere only about 0.6 percent as dense as ours. A dry, salt covered world with a full atmosphere and a Moon that has quit drifting away is something we have not seen anywhere else in the solar system.

So here is my question for you. If you could stand anywhere on the old seafloor on that first morning, with a few days before the sky runs dry, where would you walk? To the Mariana Trench, to the wreck of the Titanic, to the great ridge running down the middle of the Atlantic, or somewhere I have not mentioned? Tell me in the comments, and tell me why.
