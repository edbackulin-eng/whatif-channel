Under your boots, the ice is nearly three kilometres thick. You are standing at the South Pole. The air is thin and bone dry, and the cold is the kind that hurts to breathe. You have been told all your life that this ice is permanent. It will outlast you, your children, and theirs.

Now it is gone.

Not over centuries. Not over decades. In the time it takes you to blink, the entire Antarctic ice sheet has turned to liquid, and the surface you were standing on is falling away beneath your feet. Below you there is no ice at all. There is only dark, wet rock that has not seen open sky for millions of years.

Before we go any further, one thing has to be said plainly. This cannot happen. Nothing in nature can melt a continent of ice in an instant, and in a moment you will see just how far beyond possible it is. But we are going to keep the physics switched on for everything that follows. The flood, the weather, the oceans, and the ground itself. Near the end, the planet does something that almost nobody expects. It is not a wave and it is not a storm. It is something that happens to the shape of the Earth. Stay with me for that.

Start with the energy bill, because it is the reason this is a thought experiment and not a forecast.

The Antarctic ice sheet holds more than twenty-six million cubic kilometres of ice. That is about ninety percent of all the ice on Earth, and around seventy percent of all its fresh water. Turning ice at zero degrees into water at zero degrees takes about three hundred and thirty-four kilojoules for every kilogram. Multiply that across the whole sheet and you get roughly eight trillion trillion joules.

That number means nothing until you compare it with something. Every car, furnace, power station and jet on the planet together use roughly six hundred exajoules of energy in a year. Melting Antarctica would swallow more than ten thousand years of that, every joule of it, with none wasted. And that is only the cost of the melting itself. The ice is far colder than zero, so it has to be warmed up first, which costs extra.

So when we say Antarctica melts in an instant, we are borrowing ten thousand years of human energy use and spending it in a single moment. We will pretend the heat arrives from outside the planet. If it were drawn from the air and the sea, the rest of the Earth would freeze before the first wave arrived.

Keep that number in mind. Later you will see that the real, slow way this ice goes is already under way, and the figure that describes it may surprise you.

Now meet the thing that has just vanished.

Antarctica covers about fourteen million square kilometres, which is larger than Europe. Its ice is on average more than two kilometres thick. If every drop of it ended up in the ocean, global sea level would rise by nearly fifty-eight metres. That figure comes from BedMachine, the most detailed map yet made of the rock under the ice. It already corrects for the fact that some of the ice sits below sea level and is already pushing water aside.

Almost all of that comes from the vast eastern dome. The western sheet holds a little over four metres of sea level. That sounds minor, and I want you to remember why it is not, because the west is where the real story turns. Its bed lies far below sea level, shaped like a bowl, and a bowl full of ice with an open rim to the ocean is a fragile thing. The map also shows a canyon under Denman Glacier in the east that drops about three and a half kilometres below sea level. It is the deepest known point on land anywhere on Earth. Until now it was filled to the brim with ice.

Seconds.

Ice is less dense than water, so when it melts it shrinks. The more than twenty-six million cubic kilometres of ice become about twenty-four million cubic kilometres of liquid, a loss of about eight percent. Spread evenly across the continent, that would be a lake more than one and a half kilometres deep.

It does not stay spread. The ground under the ice is a rough landscape of mountains, trenches and basins, and water finds the low parts first. In the first seconds the meltwater drains into the deepest hollows, fills them, and begins to climb out. Then it starts to run toward the coast, down slopes that were hidden for millions of years.

The coldest air temperature ever measured by a thermometer at ground level is minus eighty-nine point two degrees Celsius, recorded at Vostok Station in Antarctica in 1983. Pour water at zero degrees into air that cold, and you get a roiling fog that blankets the entire continent. You cannot see. You can only hear rushing water in every direction.

But the first sign of trouble beyond Antarctica is not the water at all. It is something quieter that moves faster. In a moment, the sea itself starts to move before a drop of meltwater has reached it.

Minutes.

The ice sheet was not just big. It was massive, more than twenty million billion tonnes of it, and mass pulls on things. Gravity is weak, but that much mass tugs ocean water toward it. The sea surface near a great ice sheet stands higher than it otherwise would, by some tens of metres. When the ice disappears, that pull disappears with it, and changes in gravity travel at the speed of light. So before the flood has even left the coast, the sea around Antarctica has begun to slide away from the continent.

Then the flood itself arrives. Try to picture the volume. The Amazon carries more water than any other river on Earth, roughly two hundred thousand cubic metres every second. At that rate, delivering twenty-four million cubic kilometres would take nearly four thousand years. The Southern Ocean receives that in the first stretch of a day.

And it does not stay in the south. A tsunami in the deep ocean can travel at up to roughly eight hundred kilometres an hour, about the speed of a jet airliner. This surge is not a normal tsunami, but it still moves as a long ocean wave, and that sets a clock for the rest of the planet. The South Pole is about ten thousand kilometres from the equator. Do the sum, and you will see why nobody gets much warning.

Hours.

At that speed, ten thousand kilometres takes about twelve hours. So within half a day, the leading edge has reached the equator, and by the end of the first day it is spreading through the northern hemisphere. The real figure would be messier, because shallow seas and coastlines slow a wave down. But the principle holds. The planet does not get weeks to prepare.

What arrives is not a tsunami as you have seen one on the news. A tsunami is a pulse. It comes ashore, it drains back, and the sea returns to where it was. This is different. This is a step. The sea comes up, and it stays up. Walls and breakwaters make no difference, because the water simply keeps climbing over hours until it levels out.

Now comes the first honest complication, and it is a good one. The rise is not the same everywhere. That nearly fifty-eight metres is a global average, and no coastline is exactly average. Because of that vanished gravitational pull, the sea level near Antarctica ends up falling, even as the water is added to the world. The further you are from the melting ice, the more water you get. A well-known study of the western sheet alone found that the coasts of North America would see roughly a quarter more rise than the global average. So for the places most people live, the real number is higher than the headline.

That raises a question. What does a figure like fifty-eight metres actually mean for the places where people live? The answer is not what the maps in your head suggest.

Days.

Most of us picture sea level rise as a few steps up a beach. Fifty-eight metres is the height of a twenty-storey building.

More than six hundred million people already live on land less than ten metres above sea level. They go first, and fast. The Maldives, whose highest natural point is only about two and a half metres above the sea, simply stop existing as a country. About a quarter of the Netherlands already lies below sea level, so there is no chance of holding the line there either.

Florida has a highest natural point of about one hundred and five metres, which sounds like safety. But its average elevation is only about thirty metres. That means the peninsula you know is mostly gone, and what remains is a thin ridge of high ground. Everything you can name along a coast, ports, airports, refineries, power stations, was built at the water's edge because that is where trade happens. Now it is under water, and so is the infrastructure behind it.

Salt water pushes inland through the ground, too, spoiling wells and farmland far from the new shoreline. Rivers back up. Deltas, which are the most densely farmed and populated land on Earth, are the first to drown.

But the most important change over the coming weeks is in the ocean itself. There is a clue in the numbers. The world's oceans hold about one point three billion cubic kilometres of water. Adding twenty-four million cubic kilometres of fresh meltwater is an increase of about one point eight percent. That sounds small. In the places where it matters, it is not.

Weeks and months.

Fresh water floats on salt water. Around Antarctica, the freshwater lid we just created sits on top of the ocean where cold, salty, dense water normally sinks and feeds the deep currents of the entire world. That sinking is one of the great engines of ocean circulation. A study published in the journal Nature in 2023 projected that this deep overturning could slow by around forty percent by 2050 under a high-emissions path, just from the meltwater already being released. Our instant melt would be vastly more than that. Deep water would stop being renewed, and what that does to ocean oxygen and to heat stored in the abyss is a subject where scientists openly disagree on the details.

There is also the matter of colour. Fresh snow reflects eighty to ninety percent of the sunlight that hits it. Open ocean reflects only a small fraction, and dark bare rock not much more. The brightest region on Earth has just become one of the darkest. That means more sunlight absorbed at the bottom of the world, and a planet that warms a little more from the change alone.

The contested part is how all of this plays out for the climate. Some models say the loss of the polar cold engine would reshape the winds and storm tracks of the whole southern hemisphere. Others find the effects partly cancel. Honest answer: nobody knows the size, only the direction.

Now, you may be wondering when I am going to explain the part about the real world. That comes next, and it is where the timeline stops being a fantasy.

Years.

Everything so far was the thought experiment. Now take the physics out for a walk at its real speed.

Satellites and field surveys put together by an international team called IMBIE found that Antarctica lost about two thousand seven hundred and twenty billion tonnes of ice between 1992 and 2017. That raised global sea level by seven point six millimetres. More worrying, the loss rate in the five years to 2017 was about two hundred and nineteen billion tonnes a year, several times higher than in the early years of the record.

Here is the figure I promised to come back to. Divide the whole sheet by that rate and you get more than a hundred thousand years. At today's pace, Antarctica would take longer than all of human history to melt. And if that were the end of the story, there would be little to worry about.

It is not the end of the story. Melting is not constant. In West Antarctica, the bed slopes downward toward the interior, and warm ocean water can get under the floating edge of the ice and eat it away from below. This is called marine ice sheet instability, and once it starts, it feeds itself. The glacier most watched for this is Thwaites, about the size of Florida, holding enough ice to raise sea level by roughly sixty-five centimetres. Researchers on the International Thwaites Glacier Collaboration warn that if it goes, it could drag its neighbours with it, for a total approaching three metres.

How fast is deeply contested. A 2016 paper in Nature, by Robert DeConto and David Pollard, included a mechanism where towering ice cliffs collapse under their own weight. It suggested Antarctica could add more than a metre of sea level by 2100 under the highest emissions, and about fifteen metres by 2500. A later paper in 2019 reran the models against more of the geological record and found that the extreme cliff collapse was not needed to match the past, and the projected rise was much lower. Both teams are serious. The honest summary is that the near-term speed is uncertain, and the long-term direction is not.

There is evidence the ice can move fast. About fourteen thousand six hundred years ago, sea level rose roughly fourteen to eighteen metres in under five hundred years, an event called meltwater pulse 1A. Where that water came from, including how much came from Antarctica, is still being argued.

On the longer view, modelling studies of burning all known fossil fuel reserves find the Antarctic ice sheet could be nearly eliminated, over thousands of years. So the real timeline for this video is not seconds. It is centuries for the first metres and millennia for the rest.

If you think that sounds like an unusual place for the Earth to end up, you are right. It has been there before.

Thousands of years.

Antarctica did not always wear ice. Around thirty-four million years ago, the continent tipped into glaciation for the first time, in a shift called the Eocene to Oligocene transition. Before that, it was a green and wooded place. Pollen from palm trees has been pulled out of seabed cores off the Antarctic coast, dated to around fifty million years ago. Palms, near the South Pole.

More recently, around three million years ago in the Pliocene, carbon dioxide levels were roughly like today's, and sea level stood many metres higher than now. Estimates range from a few metres to more than twenty, depending on whose reconstruction you trust. That range tells you how hard it is to read old coastlines. It also tells you the planet has been in this neighbourhood before.

So the end state in our thought experiment is not an alien world. It is a very old one.

But there is a catch that nobody mentions when they show the flooded globe. The planet you end up with is not just wetter. It is different in a way the maps cannot show.

Here is the turn I promised.

All this time we have talked about the water. But the ice was also a weight. At the pole alone it is nearly three kilometres of it. The continental crust floats on a hot, slowly flowing mantle, and the ice presses it down like a person sitting on a mattress. Take the person away, and the mattress rises. Geologists call it rebound, and it is not a theory. In the Amundsen Sea region, where the ice is thinning fastest, instruments fixed on bare rock have measured the ground rising at up to forty-one millimetres a year. That is among the fastest uplift ever recorded, because the mantle beneath is unusually soft.

Now scale that up. After the whole sheet is gone, models suggest the continent could rise by hundreds of metres over thousands of years. How much depends heavily on how soft the mantle is beneath each region, which is poorly known and still contested. But the direction is certain. Antarctica springs upward, and so does the sea floor around it, and combined with the pull of gravity we talked about earlier, it means the coast of Antarctica becomes the one place on Earth where the sea has gone down, not up.

And that is only the ground. The planet itself changes its spin.

Move more than twenty million billion tonnes of mass from the bottom of the world and spread it across the oceans, much of it toward the equator. It is the same trick as a skater who spins with arms in, then reaches them out. Spread the mass out, and the spin slows. A full melt would lengthen the day by a fraction of a second. That sounds like nothing, until you learn it is already happening in miniature. A 2024 paper in Nature by the geophysicist Duncan Agnew found that melting ice in Greenland and Antarctica has slowed a speeding-up of the Earth's rotation, and delayed the point at which clock keepers might need to remove a second from world time by about three years, to 2029. The melting of Antarctica is already tugging on the clocks.

There is one more thing the ice is hiding, and you can see it in the radar maps. Beneath it lie more than four hundred lakes, liquid water sealed under kilometres of ice. And there is a mountain range, the Gamburtsev Mountains, about the size and shape of the European Alps. Nobody knew it was there until radar surveys in the late two thousands showed it buried under the ice. When the ice goes, those lakes drain and a range of Alpine peaks stands in the open for the first time since before humans existed. The mountains were sculpted by glaciers, and they will be sculpted again by rain.

So that is the end state. A continent that looks nothing like the white shape on your globe, rising out of the sea like a spring that has been let go. Coastlines all over the world redrawn. Days a little longer. An ocean slower to turn over, and a southern hemisphere that is warmer and darker than the one you were born into.

None of it happens in a blink. All of it, in some form, has happened before.

Or would you go south, to the one coast where the sea will fall, and wait for the mountains to appear? Tell me where you would stand, and why.
